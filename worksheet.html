Reading it together

A worksheet for the contract you already have

This is the exercise from Chapters 2 and 36 of the book, on one sheet. Print it, or copy the table. It takes about an hour, it needs no lawyer, and nothing on it commits either of you to anything.

How to use it

  1. Read the summaries separately. Each of you ticks your own column for every article that surprised you. Don't compare yet.
  2. Compare ticks. Don't debate. Just see where each of you was surprised. Most couples find they were surprised by different articles.
  3. Pick one article and read its chapter. Start with one that applies to every marriage: debts (5), death (9), or the federal riders (10). Chapter 10, on beneficiary forms, usually ends with both of you logging in to check a form.
  4. Decide about that one article. Keep it, change it, or ask a lawyer. “Keep” is a fine answer. Write it down anyway.
  5. Repeat until you're bored. Whatever is left under “change” and “ask” is your agenda for a one-hour meeting with a family lawyer in your state.

For the lawyer

Take the sheet and three questions to a one-hour consultation:

  1. What would the default do for us today, in this state, with our assets and incomes? Ask for a range, not a single figure.
  2. Is there anything we want that this state won't enforce?
  3. What does this state require for an agreement to hold up: disclosure, timing, separate lawyers, notarization?

Before you go, look up your state's Luck of the Draw score and its page. The full texts of the default contract and three alternatives are on the contracts page, licensed so you can copy and change them.
