The contracts

Four contracts, free to copy: the default you already have, and three you could sign instead. Paste one into a chatbot and ask it to explain an article, compare two contracts, or show what it would do to a couple like you. Or mark one up and take it to a lawyer.

Same articles as the book, in different words. These are web versions of the contracts in Appendices A and B. The article numbers and terms match the book. The wording doesn’t, so check the book if a sentence matters to you.

Treat these as places to start. They aren’t finished legal documents or legal advice, and no lawyer has reviewed them. Signing a template doesn’t make it enforceable, and each of you needs a separate lawyer licensed in your state. [Bracketed] numbers are yours to choose. Words that look like this leave a decision to a judge or arbitrator; they’re the “luck of the draw” terms.

The Marriage Contract (Default Form)

The one you already have.

 default.txt

Article 1. Parties and formation.

The parties are the two Spouses and the State. This Contract takes effect when a marriage is solemnized under a valid license. In states that allow it, it also takes effect when the Spouses presently agree to be married, then live together and are publicly known as a married couple. They acknowledge that they haven’t read this Contract and were never given a copy. Neither fact affects its validity.

In English: You’re bound. Nobody had to show you the terms first, and in a few states you didn’t even need a license.

Article 2. Governing law.

Whichever state is the domicile of either Spouse when the marriage ends by dissolution or death supplies the governing law, as it stands at that time. The place of celebration controls only whether the marriage is valid. Changing domicile may change the governing law, and either Spouse may do that without the other’s consent.

In English: The state you end up in writes the rules, as they stand on the day the marriage ends. A move can change them, and either of you can move.

Article 3. Marital property.

[Option A: 41 states and D.C., equitable distribution] Whatever either Spouse acquires from the date of marriage to the state’s cutoff, which may be separation, filing, or decree, is marital property, whoever holds title. At dissolution the court divides it in the shares it deems equitable. In most states it disregards marital misconduct. It weighs the length of the marriage; each Spouse’s age, health, income, and employability; each Spouse’s contributions, homemaking included; and any other factor it deems relevant.

[Option B: 9 states, community property] Property that either Spouse’s labor brings in during the marriage is community property, and each Spouse owns an equal undivided share from the moment it’s acquired. At dissolution the community is split equally [California, Louisiana, New Mexico] or as the court deems just and right [Texas, among others].

[Option C: all-property states] Option A applies, and the court may also divide the property described in Article 4 if it deems that equitable.

In English: What either of you earns while you’re married belongs to you both, whoever’s name is on it. Nine states treat that as half each from the start, though only three must split it evenly at a divorce. In the others it means what a judge considers fair, and a few states let the judge reach premarital and inherited property as well.

Article 4. Separate property.

A Spouse’s separate property is what that Spouse owned before the marriage or received during it by gift, bequest, or descent, plus [in most states] its passive appreciation. It turns marital, wholly or partly, to the extent it is (a) commingled beyond tracing with marital property; (b) put in joint names, which creates a presumption of a gift to the marriage; or (c) made more valuable by marital funds or either Spouse’s efforts. [Some states] Every increase in value during the marriage is marital. Whoever claims property as separate bears the burden of tracing it. Depending on the state, the standard is preponderant or clear and convincing evidence.

In English: What you owned going in stays yours, along with anything you’re given or inherit, provided you keep it fully separate and can document that years from now. Mixing it in, putting it in joint names, or spending marital money on it turns some or all of it into shared property.

Article 5. Debts.

A debt either Spouse takes on during the marriage is presumed marital and is allocated under the principles of Article 3. The allocation binds the Spouses and no creditor: a Spouse who signed an obligation stays liable on it whatever the decree says. A family expense statute or the doctrine of necessaries may make a Spouse liable to third parties for the other Spouse’s medical care and other necessities. [Community property states] Community property generally answers for debts either Spouse incurs during the marriage.

In English: Debts are divided the way assets are, but that division doesn’t bind the lender. If your ex is ordered to pay the joint card and doesn’t, the lender can still collect from you. Your spouse’s hospital bills may be yours as well.

Article 6. Support during the marriage.

Each Spouse, according to that Spouse’s own means, must support the other. No court will enforce this duty or ask whether the support is adequate while they live together. After separation, either Spouse may ask for temporary support, at a level the court deems reasonable or set by guideline where there is one.

In English: You owe each other support, but no one can enforce that before one of you moves out.

Article 7. Support after dissolution.

[Option A: most states, discretion] The court may order maintenance for either Spouse, in the amount and for the time it deems just. It considers the marriage’s length and standard of living; each Spouse’s age, health, resources, and earning capacity; what either did for the other’s career; and whatever else it deems relevant.

[Option B: formula] A statute sets maintenance by formula from the Spouses’ incomes, with a duration tied to the length of the marriage. The court may depart from these if it deems the result unjust or inappropriate.

[Option C: capped] Only a Spouse who can’t meet minimum reasonable needs, after a marriage of a stated minimum length, may receive maintenance, and it’s limited by a stated duration, share of income, or dollar amount.

[Fault rider: in a minority of states] A Spouse’s own adultery may bar that Spouse from maintenance, or the court may weigh marital misconduct in setting the amount.

Everywhere, maintenance ends when the recipient remarries or either party dies, may end or shrink when the recipient cohabits, and, unless the decree says otherwise, may be modified on a substantial change in circumstances.

In English: After a divorce, the spouse with more may owe the spouse with less. Most states can’t tell you how much or for how long until a judge decides. A few use a formula, a few limit it deliberately, and in a handful an affair can cost you your alimony.

Article 8. Children.

This Contract doesn’t govern what the Spouses owe their children, and no agreement can change those obligations. State guidelines set child support, which belongs to the child and which neither parent may waive. Legal and physical custody follow the best interests of the child, judged when the decision is made. Whatever the Spouses agree on these subjects is only advisory.

In English: Your children aren’t a term you can bargain over. A formula sets support, a judge decides custody, and anything you agreed to beforehand is a suggestion at most.

Article 9. Death.

[Option A: the majority of states] If a Spouse dies without a will, the survivor receives a share fixed by statute, from one-third to all of the estate depending on the state and on which other relatives survive. If there is a will, the survivor may reject it and elect a statutory share instead, usually one-third of the estate, or a portion that rises to as much as one-half as the marriage gets longer. The survivor may also claim a family allowance, exempt property, and homestead. Only a signed writing, following fair disclosure, can waive these rights.

[Option B: community property states] Half the community property already belongs to the survivor. By will, the decedent can dispose only of the decedent’s own half and separate property.

[Option C: Georgia] There is no elective share. The survivor may petition for a year’s support.

In English: If there’s no will, your spouse takes a big portion of the estate. If a will leaves your spouse out, your spouse can set it aside and claim a set minimum. Georgia does it differently.

Article 10. Federal riders.

(a) Tax. A joint return makes both Spouses liable, jointly and severally, for the whole tax, with limited innocent-spouse relief. Neither a transfer between Spouses nor one incident to divorce is a taxable event. Under instruments executed after 2018, the payer can’t deduct maintenance and the recipient doesn’t count it as income.

(b) Retirement plans. Each Spouse’s pension must provide the other a survivor annuity, and each Spouse is the other’s default 401(k) beneficiary. Only a spouse can waive these rights, in writing, on the plan’s form, witnessed by a notary or plan representative. A premarital agreement doesn’t count as a waiver. Dividing a plan at divorce takes a qualified domestic relations order.

(c) Beneficiary forms. An insurer or plan administrator pays whoever is named on its form, regardless of a will, a divorce decree, or this Contract. State laws that cancel a former spouse’s designation at divorce don’t reach federally governed plans.

(d) Social Security. A divorced Spouse whose marriage lasted ten years may claim benefits based on the other’s earnings.

(e) Military and federal service. Courts can divide retired pay as property. A former Spouse gets paid directly only if ten years of marriage overlapped ten years of service.

(f) Immigration. Sponsoring a Spouse for permanent residence means promising the United States to support that Spouse at 125 percent of the poverty line. Divorce doesn’t end that promise, and the Spouses can’t waive it between themselves.

(g) Bankruptcy. Bankruptcy can’t discharge support obligations. In a Chapter 7 liquidation, it also can’t discharge property-division obligations owed to a Spouse or former Spouse.

In English: Federal law adds terms that no state and no couple can delete. The ones most likely to surprise you are the joint tax return, the support promise behind a green-card sponsorship, and an old 401(k) beneficiary form.

Article 11. Disputes.

Disputes under this Contract go to the family court of the governing state, before a judge assigned without any input from the Spouses, and in nearly all states without a jury. The court has broad discretion, and an appeal succeeds only on a showing of abuse of discretion. Most disputes settle, in negotiations based on the predicted use of that discretion. Each Spouse pays that Spouse’s own fees, but the court may shift them as it deems appropriate, given how the Spouses’ resources compare or their conduct. The Spouses may agree to mediate, and in many states may agree to arbitrate financial disputes.

In English: You get one judge and no jury, and that judge has so much latitude that appeals rarely succeed. So most couples settle, which means bargaining over a forecast of what that judge would do. You each pay your own lawyer unless the judge orders otherwise.

Article 12. Amendment by the Spouses.

Together, the Spouses may change Articles 3, 4, 5, 7, or 9, and choose the governing law under Article 2, by both signing a written agreement before or during the marriage. An enforceable agreement has to be voluntary, has to follow fair and reasonable financial disclosure by each Spouse or a knowing waiver of disclosure, and can’t be unconscionable, which is judged [most states] when it’s signed or [some states] when it’s enforced. Courts look harder at agreements made during marriage. The Spouses can’t change anything in Articles 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, or 14.

In English: You two can rewrite the money terms, before or after the wedding, as long as you both sign, you’re both open about your finances, and nobody is pressured. The rules on children, federal law, the judge, and how you get out aren’t yours to rewrite.

Article 13. Amendment by the State.

Any Article may be changed at any time by the State, through statute or court decision. Unless it says otherwise, the change reaches existing marriages and dissolutions that aren’t final.

In English: Your state’s lawmakers and courts can change the deal at any time, without your consent.

Article 14. Termination.

Only a Spouse’s death or a decree of dissolution or annulment ends this Contract. The Spouses can’t end it by agreement, separation of any length doesn’t end it, and neither does breaking a promise made at the wedding. Every state lets either Spouse obtain a decree on a no-fault basis, after any waiting or separation period it requires.

In English: It ends with a death or a judge’s signature, and nothing else. Either of you can get the signature without showing the other did anything wrong. Breaking a wedding vow doesn’t breach this contract.

The Generous Contract

“What’s mine is yours,” in writing.

 generous.txt

Article 1. Purpose.

The Spouses mean to share each other’s fortunes in full. This Agreement is to be read to favor sharing over separation. In any doubtful case, it’s to be read to favor the Spouse whose income is lower or who carries more of the caregiving for children or family members (the “Supported Spouse”).

In English: If it’s unclear, we share, and we favor the lower earner or the main caregiver.

Article 2. All property is shared.

Everything either Spouse owns, whenever and however acquired, including premarital property, gifts, and inheritances, is marital property owned equally. Schedule C lists the only exceptions, such as property held for a child from a prior relationship, or heirlooms. No one may be required or allowed to trace contributions.

In English: It’s all ours: what we each brought, and whatever we inherit. Schedule C lists the only exceptions. No one ever has to hunt for old bank statements.

Article 3. Division.

At dissolution, marital property is split equally by value. The Supported Spouse may choose to take the family home as part of that Spouse’s half. If the half doesn’t cover the home, that Spouse may put off paying the difference for as long as [five] years at [the applicable federal rate].

In English: We divide everything in half. The lower earner or main caregiver can keep the house, with as long as five years to pay any difference.

Article 4. Debts.

Every debt either Spouse incurred or incurs before separation is shared equally. Exceptions: debts incurred for gambling, or to carry on or conceal an extramarital relationship, stay with the Spouse who incurred them.

In English: We share every debt, apart from what’s spent on an affair or gambling.

Article 5. Support.

At dissolution, the higher-income Spouse must pay the other [35] percent of the gap between their gross incomes, recalculated every year. Payments last as long as the marriage did or, if the marriage reached [fifteen] years, until the payer’s full Social Security retirement age. They end if the recipient remarries or either Spouse dies. Neither Spouse’s marital misconduct, and no cohabitation by the recipient, reduces them.

In English: The higher earner pays 35% of the difference in income, for as long as the marriage lasted or, after a marriage of 15 years or more, until retirement age. Fault doesn’t reduce it.

Article 6. Starting over.

Out of marital property, before it’s divided and on top of support, the Supported Spouse must receive enough to pay tuition and fees for [two] years at the governing state’s public university, for education or retraining.

In English: The spouse who stepped back from a career gets retraining money from the shared property before we divide it.

Article 7. Death.

Both Spouses keep all their rights in each other’s estates. Each must keep a will leaving the survivor at least [three-quarters] of the estate, must make the survivor the primary beneficiary of every retirement account and life insurance policy, and, during any time a child is a minor, must carry life insurance worth at least [five] times annual income.

In English: Almost everything goes to the surviving spouse. Our beneficiary forms name each other, and we keep substantial life insurance while our kids are young.

Article 8. Most-favored spouse.

If the governing state’s law at the time of enforcement would give the Supported Spouse more property or more support than this Agreement does, the law controls to that extent.

In English: Should the law ever treat the supported spouse better than this agreement does, the law applies.

Article 9. Fees and process.

Unless safety requires otherwise, the Spouses must mediate for at least [sixty] days before either files a contested motion. Marital property must pay the reasonable fees of both lawyers and the mediator before any division. If there isn’t enough liquid marital property, the higher-income Spouse must advance both Spouses’ fees.

In English: Mediation comes first. Joint funds pay both lawyers, which keeps either of us from outspending the other.

Article 10. Review.

The Spouses must review this Agreement every [fifth] anniversary and when a child is born or adopted, when they move to another state, and when either receives an inheritance. A missed review doesn’t affect validity.

In English: We go back over it every five years and after any major life event.

The No-Surprises Contract

Written to leave as little to regret as it can.

 nosurprises.txt

Article 1. Governing law.

Wherever the Spouses later live, the law of [State], as it stands on the signing date, governs their property and support rights and this Agreement.

In English: We choose one state’s rules today, and they travel with us. Most courts will respect that, but not every court will.

Article 2. Separate property.

A Spouse’s separate property is (a) the items on that Spouse’s disclosure schedule; (b) inheritances and gifts that Spouse alone received; and (c) all income and appreciation, passive or active, from the property in (a) and (b), as long as it’s held in that Spouse’s sole name. Separate property put into a joint account, or used to buy jointly titled property, turns marital, with one exception. A Spouse who puts separate funds toward buying the family home is credited with the dollars contributed, with no interest or appreciation, before the equity is divided.

In English: Keep what you brought in your own name and it stays yours, and the same goes for inheritances. Move it to a joint account and it’s shared. One exception: separate money used to buy the family home comes back to you dollar for dollar, with no growth.

Article 3. Marital property.

Everything else either Spouse acquires during the marriage is marital property. That includes equity compensation to the extent it was earned during the marriage, retirement contributions, and any increase in the value of a business in which either Spouse works. At dissolution it’s divided equally by value.

In English: Whatever either of us earns during the marriage is split evenly, including retirement contributions, stock grants, and business growth.

Article 4. Businesses and hard-to-divide assets.

Whichever Spouse works in a business may keep it by paying the other Spouse half of its marital value. A single appraiser sets that value, chosen jointly or, if the Spouses can’t agree, named by the arbitrator. Payments may stretch over as many as [five] years, with interest at [two points above the applicable federal rate] and the business interest as security.

In English: The spouse who runs the business keeps it and buys out the other, at one appraiser’s figure, over as long as five years.

Article 5. Caregiver credit.

In each calendar year when a Spouse works less than half-time, with both Spouses’ agreement, to care for their children or family members, that Spouse earns a credit for the year equal to [15] percent of what the other Spouse grossed in earned income. At dissolution, accrued credits must be paid out of the other Spouse’s share of marital property. If that share falls short, the balance becomes additional support over as long as [five] years. Credits are extinguished if either Spouse dies during the marriage.

In English: Each year one of us stays home or works part-time for the family, that spouse earns 15% of the other’s pay for the year, payable if we split. The rate is set in advance, so there’s nothing to fight about later.

Article 6. Support.

When the marriage dissolves, the Spouse with more gross income must pay the other [30] percent of the gap between their gross incomes, recalculated each year when they exchange tax returns. How long it lasts depends on the length of the marriage: under [three] years, no support; from [three] to [twenty] years, half the length of the marriage; over [twenty] years, until the payer’s full Social Security retirement age. It ends if the recipient remarries or either Spouse dies, and it’s suspended during any stretch of more than [twelve] consecutive months when the recipient cohabits with a partner.

In English: The higher earner pays 30% of the difference in income. Length depends on the marriage: no support under three years, half the marriage’s length up to twenty, and past twenty, until the payer’s retirement age.

Article 7. No fault, except with money.

Misconduct in the marriage affects no right under this Agreement, with one exception. A Spouse who, during the [three] years before filing, either spent or transferred marital property on something unrelated to the marriage, such as an extramarital relationship, or hid any asset, must pay the full amount back into the other Spouse’s share. If an asset was hidden, that Spouse also forfeits it entirely.

In English: An affair by itself doesn’t change the split. What was spent on one gets repaid, and a hidden asset goes entirely to the other spouse.

Article 8. The home.

Within [ninety] days of filing, either Spouse may purchase the other’s share of the family home, priced by appraisal. If both Spouses elect this, or neither does, the home goes on the market within [six] months, and they must share its carrying costs in proportion to income until it sells.

In English: Either of us can buy out the other’s share of the house, priced by appraisal. We sell it if neither does, or if we both want it.

Article 9. Debts.

Debts incurred for family purposes during the marriage are shared equally. Premarital debts stay with the Spouse who incurred them. Education debt incurred during the marriage stays with the Spouse who took it on, until the marriage has lasted [five] years past the date of the debt. From then on it’s shared equally.

In English: We share family debts. Debts from before the marriage stay with whoever brought them. A student loan taken out while married becomes shared once five years have passed.

Article 10. Death.

Neither Spouse gives up any right in the other’s estate. At every review under Article 12, each Spouse must give the other an up-to-date copy of every beneficiary designation.

In English: The usual rules at death still apply. Every five years we exchange copies of our beneficiary forms, which heads off most of the disasters.

Article 11. Process.

(a) Unless safety requires otherwise, a Spouse who plans to file must first give [thirty] days’ notice in writing. (b) The Spouses must attend at least [three] mediation sessions before either files anything contested on a financial issue. (c) Any financial dispute left after mediation goes to binding arbitration before one arbitrator, a family lawyer with [fifteen] or more years in practice. (d) If a Spouse rejects a written settlement offer and doesn’t then do at least [ten] percent better, that Spouse must pay the other’s fees from the date of the offer.

In English: Thirty days’ notice before anyone files. Mediation comes next. If that fails, a private arbitrator settles the money questions. Turn down a written offer and fail to beat it by the agreed margin, and you pay your spouse’s legal fees.

Article 12. Review.

The Spouses must review this Agreement every [fifth] anniversary. They must also review it no later than [six] months after a birth or adoption, a move to another state, an inheritance over [$100,000], starting or selling a business, or either Spouse’s disability. Amendments must be written, with the same formalities as this Agreement. A missed review doesn’t affect validity.

In English: We go back through it every five years and after any major change. Amendments are done properly, with signatures and lawyers.

Article 13. Hardship valve.

The arbitrator may raise the support amount or extend its length by up to [fifty] percent, as the arbitrator deems just, if a Spouse has become disabled or if, after Article 6 is applied at enforcement, a Spouse would be eligible for public assistance.

In English: The one luck-of-the-draw word in this contract is here on purpose. If one of us is disabled or destitute, the arbitrator can increase support, within a fixed limit.

The Children’s Contract

The children first, then whatever is left for the two of you to split.

 children.txt

Article 1. Purpose and priority.

At dissolution, the children’s stability, education, and security come ahead of dividing property between the Spouses. This Agreement’s provisions are to be applied in the order they appear.

In English: Our kids’ needs come before ours, and the articles apply in order.

Article 2. Children’s fund.

Before marital property is divided, the Spouses must set aside (a) for each child, enough in an education savings account to bring it to [$X] times the child’s age in years; and (b) a reserve of [twelve] months of the children’s ordinary expenses, in a joint account that needs both signatures.

In English: Before any split, each child’s college account gets funded, and a year’s worth of the kids’ expenses goes into reserve.

Article 3. The nest.

No sale of the family home may take place before the youngest child finishes secondary school, unless the Spouses both agree in writing. The parent who has the children for most overnights has the right to live there. The Spouses must share the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and major repairs in proportion to gross income, and the parent living there must pay utilities and routine upkeep. When the home is sold, net equity is split equally, appreciation after separation included.

In English: The children stay in their home until the youngest finishes high school, and the parent they’re with most of the time lives there. We split major costs by income, and the equity evenly when it finally sells.

Article 4. Two households, one standard of living.

During any time a child is a minor, the higher-income Spouse must pay whatever spousal support keeps the two households within [20] percent of each other, measured in after-tax, after-child-support income per household member. The Spouses must recalculate the amount each year when they exchange tax returns.

In English: A child shouldn’t be comfortable in one home and struggling in the other. Until the youngest is 18, support keeps the two homes within 20% of each other per person.

Article 5. Beyond guideline child support.

Child support is at least what state guidelines require. The Spouses must also split, by their shares of gross income, (a) uninsured dental, medical, and mental health bills; (b) child care; (c) summer programs, activities, and tutoring, capped at [$X] per child per year; and (d) each child’s undergraduate costs (tuition, room, and board), limited to the cost of attendance at the governing state’s flagship public university.

In English: Guideline child support is the floor. We split the extras by income and cover college at up to what an in-state public school costs. Many state courts have no power to order college payments, but parents can bind themselves by contract.

Article 6. Insurance.

Until the youngest child turns [23], each Spouse must carry (a) life insurance, payable to a trust for the children, of at least [ten] times that Spouse’s total yearly obligations under Articles 4 and 5; and (b) disability insurance if it’s reasonably available through work.

In English: The children stay provided for if either of us dies or can’t work.

Article 7. Caregiver credit.

In each year when a Spouse, with both Spouses’ agreement, works less than half-time to care for the children, that Spouse earns a credit for the year equal to [15] percent of what the other Spouse grossed in earned income. It must be paid at dissolution out of the other Spouse’s share of marital property.

In English: The No-Surprises Contract has this same credit. A parent who stays home earns a share of the other parent’s pay for those years.

Article 8. Everything else.

Marital property left after Articles 2 through 7 is split equally. Separate property stays separate, but Article 3 governs the family home no matter who holds title.

In English: What remains is split evenly. Separate property stays separate, apart from the house, which follows the children’s plan whoever’s name is on it.

Article 9. Wills and later marriages.

During any time a child is under [25], each Spouse must keep a will or trust leaving at least [one-half] of the estate to the children or for their benefit, and may not remarry without a premarital agreement that preserves that share. The children may enforce this Article.

In English: Remarrying can’t write our kids out. A new spouse must sign a prenup preserving what the kids would get from the estate.

Article 10. Relocation costs.

Whichever parent moves over [thirty] miles from the family home must pay all the added cost of getting the children between households.

In English: We can’t tell a court who may move. We can settle who buys the plane tickets: the parent who moved.

Article 11. Process.

Except in an emergency, a Spouse must attend [three] sessions with a child specialist or mediator before filing a contested motion about the children. Neither Spouse may call a child to testify, or even ask a child to pick between parents. The Spouses must pay fees for parenting disputes equally, and never from Article 2 funds.

In English: A mediator before a courtroom. No child on the witness stand, and no legal fees out of the college fund.

Article 12. The Parenting Pledge (not binding).

For each other’s guidance and any court’s, the Spouses state what they now intend after separation: (a) the children’s schedule will put stable school, friends, and activities ahead of equal time with each parent; (b) neither parent will disparage the other when the children are present; (c) each parent may attend the children’s events and will get complete information on school, medical care, and activities; (d) the parents will decide major questions of education, health, and religion together; (e) a new partner won’t meet the children until the relationship is [six] months old; and (f) before moving the children’s primary residence over [thirty] miles, a parent will get the other’s consent or a court order.

In English: This says how we mean to act. A court may ignore it. It’s here to show a judge, and to remind us on bad days, what we promised when our heads were clear.
