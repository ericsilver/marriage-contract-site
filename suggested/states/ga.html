Georgia

Your marriage contract, Georgia edition

85 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 85 out of 100

Georgia leaves property division, alimony amount and duration, and fee awards to the factfinder’s discretion, and adultery or desertion that caused the separation bars alimony. A party can ask a jury rather than the judge to decide property and alimony. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Georgia ranks 36 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. Getting in

No wait from license to wedding. Apply at a county probate court; if either partner lives in Georgia any county works, otherwise use the county of the ceremony. A license may issue immediately with no waiting period, and the sections read set no expiration date. A six-hour premarital course taken together within 12 months removes the $40 statutory fee; other local charges remain.
O.C.G.A. §§ 19-3-30(a)-(b), 19-3-30.1(a), (c), 19-3-35, 15-9-60(k)(14)-(14.1)

Who can marry you. The Governor or a former Governor, a state or federal judge, city recorder, magistrate, minister, or other person a religious society’s rules authorize may officiate. The statute sets no witness count and does not address online ordination. Two witness affidavits come up only if a license is never returned for recording.
O.C.G.A. § 19-3-30(c), (e)

Common-law marriage: Closed to new couples. No common-law marriage can be formed in Georgia on or after January 1, 1997. Those validly formed earlier are still recognized. The Georgia Supreme Court also recognizes a common-law marriage validly established under another state’s law, and the person claiming it must prove it under that state’s law.
O.C.G.A. § 19-3-1.1; Norman v. Ault, 287 Ga. 324, 695 S.E.2d 633 (2010)

The property system

Georgia is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

What counts as marital. Georgia’s rule comes from court decisions. Property acquired during the marriage is divided equitably, while premarital property, gifts and inheritances stay separate. Accumulation continues until the final divorce decree, even after separation. Growth on separate property from market forces stays separate, and growth from either spouse’s efforts is marital.
Stokes v. Stokes, 246 Ga. 765 (1980); Friedman v. Friedman, 259 Ga. 530, 384 S.E.2d 641 (1989); Bass v. Bass, 264 Ga. 506, 448 S.E.2d 366 (1994); Armour v. Holcombe, 288 Ga. 50, 701 S.E.2d 169 (2010)

An inheritance that touches a joint account

It depends on what the owner meant. Georgia courts look at what the owner intended. In one case a $10,000 gift stayed separate after deposit in a joint account, because the recipient’s spouse would not allow an individual account. A deed of separate property into both spouses’ names showed intent to make it marital, and deposit into a joint operating account counted against the owner in another case.
Bloomfield v. Bloomfield, 282 Ga. 108, 646 S.E.2d 207 (2007); Lerch v. Lerch, 278 Ga. 885, 608 S.E.2d 223 (2005); Miller v. Miller, 288 Ga. 274, 705 S.E.2d 839 (2010)

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don’t owe your spouse’s bills. No Georgia statute makes one spouse liable for the other’s necessaries, and each spouse’s separate property stays separate; the husband-liability sections of the 1933 Code (§§ 53-508 and 53-510) were not carried into the current Official Code, whose only necessaries statutes cover children.
O.C.G.A. §§ 19-3-9, 19-6-13, 19-6-14 (current Title 19 has no spousal-necessaries section); former Ga. Code 1933 §§ 53-508, 53-510

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Georgia has no elective share; the surviving spouse and minor children may claim year’s support, which is property set aside for 12 months of support, ranks ahead of all other debts of the estate, and is sized (if contested) to keep the survivor’s prior standard of living, taking account of the survivor’s own resources and the estate’s solvency. It is taken from the decedent’s estate, so non-probate assets are not reached.
O.C.G.A. §§ 53-3-1, 53-3-7

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under case law (Scherer) plus formality statutes O.C.G.A. §§ 19-3-60 to 19-3-66; Georgia has not adopted the UPAA or UPMAA. The court asks whether the agreement was obtained through fraud, duress, mistake, misrepresentation or nondisclosure of material facts; whether it is unconscionable; and whether facts and circumstances have changed since execution so as to make enforcement unfair and unreasonable. Postnuptial (reconciliation) agreements are tested by the same criteria.
Scherer v. Scherer, 249 Ga. 635 (1982); Curry v. Curry, 260 Ga. 302 (1990), as quoted in Spurlin v. Spurlin, No. S11F0864 (Ga. Oct. 3, 2011)

What an agreement needs. An antenuptial agreement must be in writing, signed by both parties, and attested by at least two witnesses, one of whom is a notary public; Scherer’s first prong requires disclosure of material facts (assets). No statute sets a waiting period, requires independent counsel, or limits alimony waivers, and an antenuptial agreement may cover year’s support, spousal support and equitable division.
O.C.G.A. §§ 19-3-60(a), 19-3-62, 19-3-63

Children (Article 8)

Child support: income shares. A schedule sets a basic obligation from both parents’ combined adjusted income, and each parent owes a pro rata share. Since January 1, 2026, a formula adjusts for court-ordered parenting time. Support ends at 18, but a judge may extend it while the child finishes secondary school, up to age 20. A court cannot order college support after 18 but can enforce parents’ agreements.
O.C.G.A. § 19-6-15(b), (e), (g), as amended by 2024 Ga. Laws S.B. 454; Wood v. Wood, 257 Ga. 598, 361 S.E.2d 819 (1987)

Moving away with a child. Unless a court order says otherwise, a parent changing residence must notify the other parent at least 30 days ahead with the full new address, and a custodial parent must also notify anyone with visitation rights. If custody is challenged, the judge decides on the child’s best interests, with no presumption for or against the moving parent.
O.C.G.A. § 19-9-3(f)(3); Bodne v. Bodne, 277 Ga. 445, 588 S.E.2d 728 (2003)

Chapter 17 covers the children.

Getting out (Article 2)

How long you must live here before filing: six months. The person filing must have been a bona fide Georgia resident for six months. Someone who lived on a U.S. military post in Georgia for one year may file in an adjacent county. A nonresident may file in the other spouse’s county if that spouse has lived in Georgia and that county for six months. The rule does not depend on where the couple married.
O.C.G.A. § 19-5-2

How long it takes. One spouse can obtain a divorce on the ground that the marriage is irretrievably broken, without proving fault and over the other’s objection; the question is whether reconciliation is possible. The court cannot grant it until 30 days after the other spouse is served. No separation period is required.
O.C.G.A. § 19-5-3(13); Harwell v. Harwell, 233 Ga. 89 (1974)

The legal minimum for a spouse who moves here alone and files as soon as allowed, with no minor children and the other spouse objecting, is about 210 days from arrival to a final decree (180 days of residence + 30-day wait after service). Courts often take longer.
