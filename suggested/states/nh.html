New Hampshire

Your marriage contract, New Hampshire edition

65 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 65 out of 100

New Hampshire presumes an equal split of all property either spouse owns, including property from before the marriage, and sets term alimony by a formula of 23 percent of the income gap for no more than half the length of the marriage. Judges can depart from both when justice requires, and fees shift only on a showing of need and ability to pay. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” New Hampshire ranks 15 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

New Hampshire is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

The spouse who got the care pays first. The New Hampshire Supreme Court made the necessaries doctrine reciprocal between spouses and held that the spouse who received the goods or services is primarily liable and the other spouse is liable only if the recipient’s resources are insufficient; the creditor must first seek payment from the recipient.
Cheshire Medical Center v. Holbrook, 140 N.H. 187 (1995)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

A surviving spouse who waives the will takes, from the estate left after debts and administration expenses, one-third of the personalty and realty if the decedent left issue; if no issue but a parent or sibling, $10,000 of each plus one-half of the remainder; if neither, $10,000 plus $2,000 per full year of marriage plus one-half of the remainder; the share is computed on the probate estate.
N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 560:10

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under own short statute (RSA 460:2-a) plus case law (MacFarlane 1989; Hollett 2003). A prenuptial agreement is presumed valid unless the challenger proves it was obtained through fraud, duress, mistake, misrepresentation or nondisclosure of a material fact, that it is unconscionable, or that the facts and circumstances have so changed since execution that enforcement would work an unconscionable hardship beyond the parties’ contemplation.
N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 460:2-a; MacFarlane v. Rich, 132 N.H. 608 (1989); In re Estate of Hollett, 150 N.H. 39 (2003)

What an agreement needs. The contract must be written and may not abrogate the statutory or common-law rights of the couple’s minor children; no notarization, witnesses, waiting period, or counsel requirement is set by statute, but in Hollett an agreement first shown to the bride less than 48 hours before the wedding, reviewed with a lawyer arranged and paid for by the groom’s side the day before, was set aside for duress.
N.H. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 460:2-a; In re Estate of Hollett, 150 N.H. 39 (2003)
