Arkansas

Your marriage contract, Arkansas edition

80 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 80 out of 100

Arkansas presumes an equal split of marital property, but the judge may depart with written reasons and may also divide premarital, gifted, or inherited property. Alimony amount and duration, and fee awards, are left to the judge’s discretion, and a party cannot remove the assigned judge or ask for a jury. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Arkansas ranks 30 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Arkansas is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don’t owe your spouse’s bills. The statute abolishes the common-law doctrine of necessaries and provides that, absent express authority, neither spouse is liable for the other’s debts, including debts for necessaries such as medicine.
Ark. Code Ann. § 9-11-516

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Arkansas keeps dower and curtesy instead of an elective share. A spouse married more than one year may take against the will and receive dower or curtesy as in intestacy: with children, a life estate in one-third of the lands the decedent owned at any time during the marriage (unless the survivor relinquished it) plus one-third of the personal estate outright; with no children, one-half against collateral heirs and one-third against creditors.
Ark. Code Ann. §§ 28-39-401, 28-11-301, 28-11-305, 28-11-307

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), Arkansas Premarital Agreement Act, Ark. Code Ann. § 9-11-401 et seq.. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if not signed voluntarily, or if unconscionable when signed and the challenger lacked fair disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing after consulting counsel, and did not otherwise know the other’s finances. Postnuptial agreements are analyzed under contract law and closely scrutinized to ensure they are fair and equitable, with full disclosure of assets.
Ark. Code Ann. § 9-11-406; Stewart v. Combs, 368 Ark. 121, 243 S.W.3d 294 (2006)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed and acknowledged by both parties. Acknowledgment means a declaration before a notary or other officer, a sworn affirmation by each party’s lawyer, a notarized agreement stating each party consulted a lawyer and signed freely, or signing before two disinterested witnesses. A written waiver of disclosure counts only if made after consulting counsel, and a court may order support despite a waiver that would leave a spouse on public assistance.
Ark. Code Ann. § 9-11-402; § 9-11-406(a)(2)(ii), (b)
