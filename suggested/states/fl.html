Florida

Your marriage contract, Florida edition

55 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 55 out of 100

Florida sets the judge’s starting point for property as an equal split and, since July 2023, caps how long every type of alimony lasts and how much durational alimony can be. Judges keep wide discretion over attorney’s fees, and adultery can affect alimony. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Florida ranks 8 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. Getting in

3 days from license to wedding. Florida residents wait 3 days after applying unless both complete a 4-hour premarital course, which also lowers the fee. Non-residents and couples asserting hardship are exempt, and a county judge may waive the wait for good cause. The license is good for 60 days. Both must confirm they read the state’s family law handbook.
Fla. Stat. §§ 741.01, 741.02, 741.04, 741.041, 741.0305

Who can marry you. Ordained ministers, elders in communion with a church, other ordained clergy, judicial officers including retired ones, circuit court clerks, and Florida notaries public may officiate. Marriages among Quakers in their own form are valid. The statute does not address online ordination and does not require witnesses.
Fla. Stat. § 741.07

Common-law marriage: Closed to new couples. No common-law marriage entered into after January 1, 1968 is valid in Florida, apart from a defective marriage entered in good faith and in substantial compliance with the marriage chapter. Florida respects a common-law marriage validly created in a jurisdiction that recognizes them.
Fla. Stat. § 741.211; American Airlines, Inc. v. Mejia, 766 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000)

The property system

Florida is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

What counts as marital. Assets acquired during the marriage are presumed marital, including gifts between spouses. Premarital assets and third-party gifts and inheritances stay nonmarital. Their growth is marital if caused by a spouse’s effort or marital funds, and paying a mortgage with marital funds can reach passive growth too. The cutoff is a valid separation agreement or filing, whichever comes first.
Fla. Stat. § 61.075(6)-(8)

An inheritance that touches a joint account

Putting it in joint names usually makes it marital. Inherited money put in a joint account with other funds so it can no longer be traced is presumed a gift of half to the other spouse. Property titled as tenants by the entireties is presumed marital, and overcoming that gift presumption takes clear and convincing evidence. Courts have kept funds nonmarital where a spouse’s name was added only for convenience.
Fla. Stat. § 61.075(6)(a)3.-4.; Williams v. Williams, 686 So. 2d 805 (Fla. 4th DCA 1997); Lakin v. Lakin, 901 So. 2d 186 (Fla. 4th DCA 2005); Grieco v. Grieco, 917 So. 2d 1052 (Fla. 2d DCA 2006); Valentine v. Van Sickle, 42 So. 3d 267 (Fla. 2d DCA 2010)

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don’t owe your spouse’s bills. The Florida Supreme Court abrogated the common-law doctrine of necessaries and left the policy to the legislature, so neither spouse is liable for the other’s medical or other necessary bills because of the marriage; no Florida statute replacing the doctrine was found.
Connor v. Southwest Florida Regional Medical Center, Inc., 668 So. 2d 175 (Fla. 1995)

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect 30 percent of the elective estate, which includes the probate estate, protected homestead, pay-on-death and joint accounts, joint property, revocable trusts and other non-probate transfers.
Fla. Stat. §§ 732.2065, 732.2035

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983) with a Florida addition, Fla. Stat. § 61.079. A premarital agreement is unenforceable in a family-law case if not signed voluntarily, if the product of fraud, duress, coercion or overreaching, or if unconscionable when signed without fair disclosure. A postnuptial agreement may be set aside for fraud, deceit, duress, coercion, misrepresentation or overreaching, or if it makes unfair or unreasonable provision for the challenger given the parties’ circumstances and was made without adequate knowledge of the other’s finances.
Fla. Stat. § 61.079(7)(a); Casto v. Casto, 508 So. 2d 330 (Fla. 1987)

What an agreement needs. A premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties, and a court may order support despite a waiver that would leave a spouse on public assistance. A waiver of elective-share, homestead and other death rights must be signed in the presence of two subscribing witnesses, and it requires fair disclosure only if signed after the marriage.
Fla. Stat. § 61.079(3), (7)(b); Fla. Stat. § 732.702(1), (2)

Children (Article 8)

Child support: income shares. Each parent’s share comes from combined net income run through a statutory schedule. Support ends at 18, or continues for a dependent high school student expected to graduate before 19. A judge may not order college costs merely because a child is in college and a parent can pay; it takes legal dependence or a binding agreement.
Fla. Stat. §§ 61.13(1)(a)1.a., 61.30(1), (5), (6), (9), 743.07(2); Grapin v. Grapin, 450 So. 2d 853 (Fla. 1984)

Moving away with a child. A parent moving at least 50 miles for 60 or more consecutive days needs a signed agreement or must file and serve a petition. No fixed advance-notice period applies, but the other parent has 20 days to object, and a timely objection blocks the move until a court allows it. The parent moving must first prove best interests; the burden then shifts.
Fla. Stat. § 61.13001(1)(e), (2), (3), (7), (8)

Chapter 17 covers the children.

Getting out (Article 2)

How long you must live here before filing: six months. One spouse must reside in Florida for 6 months before the petition is filed. It can be either spouse. The statute does not depend on where the couple married or where the breakdown happened. The residence must be corroborated, for example by a Florida driver license, ID card, voter card, or a third party’s testimony or affidavit.
Fla. Stat. §§ 61.021, 61.052(2)

How long it takes. A judge grants a divorce on finding the marriage irretrievably broken, even if the other spouse denies it. No final judgment may be entered until 20 days after filing, unless the delay would cause injustice. If the other spouse denies the breakdown or there is a minor child, the judge may order counseling or continue the case up to 3 months.
Fla. Stat. §§ 61.052(1)-(2), 61.19

The legal minimum for a spouse who moves here alone and files as soon as allowed, with no minor children and the other spouse objecting, is about 200 days from arrival to a final decree (180 days of residence + 20-day wait). Courts often take longer.
