Delaware

Your marriage contract, Delaware edition

70 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 70 out of 100

Delaware lets the judge decide how to split marital property, how much alimony to pay and who pays legal fees, but keeps inherited, gifted and premarital property out of the division and bars any consideration of marital misconduct. Alimony is available only to a dependent spouse and, for marriages under 20 years, can last no more than half the length of the marriage. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Delaware ranks 18 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Delaware is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Delaware’s statute places the duty to support a spouse on the other spouse in gender-neutral terms, but no Delaware decision was found that says whether, or in what order, a hospital or other creditor may collect one spouse’s necessary bills from the other spouse.
13 Del. C. § 502 (not yet checked against the source)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect one-third of the elective estate, less all transfers to the survivor from the decedent. The elective estate is the decedent’s federal gross estate less debts and expenses, so it reaches non-probate assets such as joint property, trusts and life insurance.
12 Del. C. §§ 901(a), 902(a)

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), 13 Del. C. §§ 321-328. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if not signed voluntarily, or if unconscionable when signed and the challenger lacked fair and reasonable disclosure, did not waive it in writing, and did not otherwise know the other’s finances. A written waiver of alimony made before, during or after the marriage bars an alimony claim.
13 Del. C. § 326; 13 Del. C. § 1512(f)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties and needs no consideration; the act has no notary, witness, waiting-period or counsel requirement. Disclosure matters through the unconscionability defense, and Delaware’s act omits the UPAA clause that lets a court override a support waiver that would leave a spouse on public assistance.
13 Del. C. §§ 322, 326
