Vermont

Your marriage contract, Vermont edition

80 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 80 out of 100

Vermont lets the judge divide all property of either spouse, whenever acquired, and weigh fault in doing so. Maintenance follows a guideline table that the judge treats as one factor among several, and fault may not be used in setting maintenance. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Vermont ranks 30 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Vermont is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don’t owe your spouse’s bills. The Vermont Supreme Court abolished the common-law necessaries doctrine rather than extend it to wives, leaving the question to the Legislature, and no family-expense statute makes a spouse liable; by statute a wife’s separate property is not liable for her husband’s debts.
Medical Center Hospital of Vermont v. Lorrain, 165 Vt. 12, 675 A.2d 1326 (1996); 15 V.S.A. § 66

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may waive the will and take one-half of the balance of the probate estate after allowances, claims and expenses; non-probate assets are not included.
14 V.S.A. § 319

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under case law only. A prenuptial agreement is enforced if each spouse made fair and reasonable financial disclosure, entered it voluntarily, and its property terms are fair; unconscionability is measured by the circumstances at signing. Even a valid agreement will not be enforced if at divorce it would leave a spouse a public charge or close to it.
Bassler v. Bassler, 156 Vt. 353, 593 A.2d 82 (1991); Stalb v. Stalb, 168 Vt. 235, 719 A.2d 421 (1998); Lacroix v. Rysz, 2025 VT 16

What an agreement needs. An agreement made in consideration of civil marriage must be in writing and signed by the party to be charged; no statute requires notarization, witnesses, a waiting period or counsel for a prenup’s divorce terms. A written waiver of the elective share and other death rights, before or during marriage, must be signed by the waiving spouse and is invalid if involuntary, unconscionable when signed or in application after a later material change, made without fair disclosure of the decedent’s finances, or made without an opportunity for meaningful access to independent counsel.
12 V.S.A. § 181(3); 14 V.S.A. § 323
