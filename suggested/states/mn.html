Minnesota

Your marriage contract, Minnesota edition

50 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 50 out of 100

Minnesota judges divide marital property as they find fair and set the amount of maintenance by weighing factors, but they may not consider marital misconduct for either. Since August 2024 the length of maintenance follows presumptions tied to how long the marriage lasted, and each party may remove the assigned judge once without giving a reason. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Minnesota ranks 5 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Minnesota is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don’t owe your spouse’s bills. Since October 1, 2024, a spouse is not liable to a creditor for any debts of the other spouse; the Legislature deleted the former rule that spouses living together were jointly and severally liable for necessary medical services and household necessaries, though a divorce court may still apportion such debt and creditors may claim against a decedent’s estate.
Minn. Stat. § 519.05, as amended by 2024 Minn. Laws ch. 114, art. 3, § 83 (eff. Oct. 1, 2024)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect a percentage of the augmented estate that rises with the length of the marriage, from a supplemental amount only (under one year) through 3 percent at one year to 50 percent at 15 years or more, with a $75,000 supplemental floor; the augmented estate includes the net probate estate, nonprobate transfers to others and to the spouse, and the spouse’s own property.
Minn. Stat. §§ 524.2-202, 524.2-203

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under own statute (Minn. Stat. § 519.11, rewritten 2024). An agreement must be procedurally and substantively fair; the court asks whether all or part of it is so substantively unfair as to be unconscionable either by its terms or because of drastically changed circumstances not foreseen when it was made, such that enforcement no longer matches the parties’ reasonable expectations at signing. Postnuptial agreements are valid only if each spouse had separate legal counsel at execution, and one is presumed unenforceable if a separation or dissolution action begins within two years unless the enforcing spouse proves it fair and equitable.
Minn. Stat. § 519.11, subds. 1c, 1d (2024 Minn. Laws ch. 101, art. 3, § 1)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing, executed in the presence of two witnesses, and acknowledged before a notary or other officer authorized to administer oaths (a power of attorney does not satisfy this); it requires full and fair disclosure of income and property, which cannot be waived, a meaningful opportunity to consult independent counsel, and voluntary execution. An agreement signed at least seven days before the wedding is presumed enforceable; one signed later loses the presumption and the proponent bears the burden of proof. No statutory public-assistance override exists; spousal-maintenance terms are tested under the unconscionability standard.
Minn. Stat. § 519.11, subds. 1b, 1c
