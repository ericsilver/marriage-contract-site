Rhode Island

Your marriage contract, Rhode Island edition

95 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 95 out of 100

Rhode Island leaves property shares, alimony and fees to the family court’s weighing of factors, and marital conduct is a listed factor for both property and alimony. The one limit is that premarital, gifted and inherited property cannot be assigned. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Rhode Island ranks 45 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Rhode Island is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

The only Rhode Island authority located is a 1957 decision in which a hospital extended credit on the common-law rule that a husband is liable for his wife’s necessary hospital care; a statute says the wife and her property are not liable for her husband’s contracts, and no case or statute making the rule gender-neutral was found.
Memorial Hospital v. Woolf, 86 R.I. 357 (1957); R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-4-12 (not yet checked against the source)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Rhode Island has no fractional elective share; the surviving spouse takes a life estate in the real estate the decedent owned at death, may renounce a will within six months to claim it, and has no life estate in land the decedent conveyed by a deed recorded before death; personal property and non-probate assets are not reached.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 33-25-2, 33-25-4

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), modified. Rhode Island changed the UPAA’s ’or’ to ’and’: the challenger must prove by clear and convincing evidence both that the agreement was signed involuntarily and that it was unconscionable when signed with inadequate disclosure, no written waiver, and no adequate knowledge. The Act covers only premarital agreements; the standard for postnuptial agreements was not confirmed from a primary source.
R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-17-6; Penhallow v. Penhallow, 649 A.2d 1016 (R.I. 1994)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; no notarization, witnesses, waiting period or independent counsel is required, and disclosure matters only through the enforcement test. The agreement may modify or eliminate spousal support, but a court may order support if the waiver would make a party eligible for public assistance.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-17-2, 15-17-3(a)(4), 15-17-6(c)
