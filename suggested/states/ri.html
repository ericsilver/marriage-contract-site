Rhode Island

Your marriage contract, Rhode Island edition

95 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 95 out of 100

Rhode Island leaves property shares, alimony and fees to the family court’s weighing of factors, and marital conduct is a listed factor for both property and alimony. The one limit is that premarital, gifted and inherited property cannot be assigned. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Rhode Island ranks 45 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. Getting in

No waiting period. Both people apply in person at a city or town clerk’s office where either lives, or where the ceremony will occur if neither lives in Rhode Island. The statutes read set no waiting period. A divorced applicant must bring an authenticated decree. The statute makes the license valid for three months; state health guidance says 90 days.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-2-1, 15-2-8, 15-2-9, 15-2-9.1

Who can marry you. Ordained clergy or elders in good standing, many current and former judges and court clerks, and certain legislative clerks may officiate before two witnesses. The governor designates, on application, anyone 18 or older for one named marriage. Quakers, Jews and Baha’is may marry under their own practices. The statute does not mention online ordination; the Secretary of State says online-ordained ministers may perform marriages.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-3-5, 15-3-6, 15-3-8; Secretary of State One-Day Marriage Officiant FAQs

Common-law marriage: A couple can still become married here without a license. A couple can still form a common-law marriage in Rhode Island. The person claiming one must show by clear and convincing evidence that both seriously intended to be married and acted so that their community believed they were. The sources read do not address one formed in another state directly; a statute honors out-of-state legal unions that carry the same rights as marriage.
DeMelo v. Zompa, 844 A.2d 174 (R.I. 2004); Fravala v. City of Cranston, 996 A.2d 696 (R.I. 2010); R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-1-8

The property system

Rhode Island is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

What counts as marital. Assets acquired up to the final divorce decree are marital, even after separation or filing, unless the spouses expressly agreed to an earlier date. Property held before the marriage, inheritances and gifts from third parties cannot be assigned to the other spouse. A judge may assign income from premarital property, and its appreciation during the marriage if either spouse’s efforts caused the increase.
R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-5-16.1(b); Ruffel v. Ruffel, 900 A.2d 1178 (R.I. 2006); Vanni v. Vanni, 535 A.2d 1268 (R.I. 1988)

An inheritance that touches a joint account

Putting it in joint names usually makes it marital. The statute bars assigning an inheritance, but moving nonmarital money into joint names is understood as a transfer to the marital estate unless clear and convincing evidence shows otherwise. Adding a spouse’s name only for convenience does not transmute it when the judge finds no intent to give. Inherited and other money that is commingled and exchanged for other property becomes marital.
R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-5-16.1(b); Olivieri v. Olivieri, 760 A.2d 1246 (R.I. 2000), quoting Quinn v. Quinn, 512 A.2d 848 (R.I. 1986); Stephenson v. Stephenson, 811 A.2d 1138 (R.I. 2002)

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

The only Rhode Island authority located is a 1957 decision in which a hospital extended credit on the common-law rule that a husband is liable for his wife’s necessary hospital care; a statute says the wife and her property are not liable for her husband’s contracts, and no case or statute making the rule gender-neutral was found.
Memorial Hospital v. Woolf, 86 R.I. 357 (1957); R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-4-12 (not yet checked against the source)

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Rhode Island has no fractional elective share; the surviving spouse takes a life estate in the real estate the decedent owned at death, may renounce a will within six months to claim it, and has no life estate in land the decedent conveyed by a deed recorded before death; personal property and non-probate assets are not reached.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 33-25-2, 33-25-4

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), modified. Rhode Island changed the UPAA’s ’or’ to ’and’: the challenger must prove by clear and convincing evidence both that the agreement was signed involuntarily and that it was unconscionable when signed with inadequate disclosure, no written waiver, and no adequate knowledge. The Act covers only premarital agreements; the standard for postnuptial agreements was not confirmed from a primary source.
R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-17-6; Penhallow v. Penhallow, 649 A.2d 1016 (R.I. 1994)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; no notarization, witnesses, waiting period or independent counsel is required, and disclosure matters only through the enforcement test. The agreement may modify or eliminate spousal support, but a court may order support if the waiver would make a party eligible for public assistance.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-17-2, 15-17-3(a)(4), 15-17-6(c)

Children (Article 8)

Child support: income shares. Rhode Island uses an income shares model under a Family Court guideline, and a judge who finds the result inequitable may set a different amount with findings. Support ends at 18. A judge may continue it for a child in high school at 18, up to 90 days after graduation but not past 19, and longer for a severely impaired child. The statute has no college provision.
R.I. Gen. Laws § 15-5-16.2(a), (b); Family Court Form FC-78; Hovarth v. Craddock, No. 2002-299-Appeal (R.I. 2003)

Moving away with a child. The Rhode Island custody statute read sets no notice period or general relocation test. A judge decides a contested move by the child’s best interests, weighing eight factors from the Dupré decision, including the child’s relationships and the reasons for the move. The moving parent need not show a compelling reason, and no single factor controls.
Dupré v. Dupré, 857 A.2d 242 (R.I. 2004); Leon v. Krikorian (R.I. 2022); Dawson v. Ojeda (R.I. 2023)

Getting out (Article 2)

How long you must live here before filing: one year. The filing spouse must have been domiciled in and lived in Rhode Island for one year immediately before filing. If the other spouse meets that test instead and is actually served, the filer’s own residence does not matter. The complaint goes in the county where the filer lives, with no minimum time there. Where the marriage took place does not change this.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-5-12(a), 15-5-13

How long it takes. A divorce for irreconcilable differences is granted whatever the fault, with no separation period, and the statute does not require both spouses to agree. Unless the court orders otherwise, trial cannot start for 60 days after filing, and judgment is not final until three months after the decision. A divorce after three years apart becomes final after 20 days.
R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 15-5-3, 15-5-3.1, 15-5-23(a); R.I. Fam. Ct. R. Dom. Rel. P. 40(a)

The legal minimum for a spouse who moves here alone and files as soon as allowed, with no minor children and the other spouse objecting, is about 515 days from arrival to a final decree (365 days of residence + 60 before trial + 90 after decision). Courts often take longer.
