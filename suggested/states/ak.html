Alaska

Your marriage contract, Alaska edition

50 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 50 out of 100

Alaska leaves the amount and length of maintenance to the judge’s weighing of factors, and the judge can reach premarital property when fairness requires it. Property division starts from an equal split, marital fault is excluded by statute, fee awards follow the spouses’ relative finances, and either side can replace the assigned judge once without giving a reason. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Alaska ranks 5 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Alaska is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

No Alaska statute makes a spouse liable for the other’s medical or other necessary bills, and the statute says neither spouse is liable for the other’s premarital or separate debts. Couples who opt into community property by agreement or trust are governed by the Alaska Community Property Act instead.
AS 25.15.050; AS 25.15.010; AS 34.77 (not yet checked against the source)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect one-third of the augmented estate, which includes the net probate estate and the decedent’s non-probate transfers to others (and counts the survivor’s own property and what the survivor receives), plus a supplemental amount that brings the survivor up to $50,000.
AS 13.12.202; AS 13.12.203

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under case law only (Brooks v. Brooks); Alaska has not adopted the UPAA or UPMAA. The court asks whether the agreement was voluntary, then whether it was unconscionable when signed or whether circumstances have changed since signing so that enforcement would be unfair and unreasonable. A court may strike individual terms without voiding the whole agreement; in Kilkenny the court struck the bars on spousal support, attorney’s fees and an unequal property division after the husband’s domestic violence.
Brooks v. Brooks, 733 P.2d 1044 (Alaska 1987); Kilkenny v. Kilkenny, No. S-18602, Op. No. 7787 (Alaska Sept. 19, 2025)

What an agreement needs. An agreement made in consideration of marriage must be in writing and signed by the party charged; no statute requires notarization, witnesses, a waiting period or counsel. A waiver of the elective share must be in writing and signed, and it fails if involuntary or if unconscionable when signed without fair disclosure. An opt-in community property agreement must be signed by both spouses and begin with a statutory warning in capital letters.
AS 09.25.010(a)(5); AS 13.12.213; AS 34.77.090
