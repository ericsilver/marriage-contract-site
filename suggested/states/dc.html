District of Columbia

Your marriage contract, District of Columbia edition

95 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 95 out of 100

D.C. leaves nearly all of the money to the judge: marital property is split on an open-ended fairness standard, alimony amount and length follow only a list of factors, and the reasons for the breakup may count in both. Premarital, gifted and inherited property stays with its owner. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” District of Columbia ranks 45 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

District of Columbia is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what’s in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. By statute both spouses (and both domestic partners) are liable on any debt either incurs during the marriage or partnership for necessaries for either of them or for their dependent children.
D.C. Code § 46-601(b)(2)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

A surviving spouse or domestic partner may renounce the will within six months of probate and take the share he or she would have received in intestacy, capped at one-half of the net estate passing under the will. The share reaches only the probate estate, and dower and curtesy are abolished.
D.C. Code § 19-113(a), (e); D.C. Code § 19-102

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can’t take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), D.C. Code § 46-501 et seq. (extended to domestic partners). A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if not signed voluntarily, or if unconscionable when signed and the challenger lacked fair and reasonable disclosure, did not waive it in writing, and did not otherwise know the other’s finances. The Code treats a valid antenuptial or postnuptial agreement as controlling death rights, but no statutory enforcement standard for postnuptial agreements was found.
D.C. Code § 46-506(a); D.C. Code § 19-113(f)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties and needs no consideration; the act has no notary, witness, waiting-period or counsel requirement. Disclosure matters through the unconscionability defense, and a court may order support despite a waiver that would leave a spouse or partner eligible for public assistance.
D.C. Code § 46-502; § 46-506(b)
