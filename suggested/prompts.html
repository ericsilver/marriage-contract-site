Prompts for a Chatbot

What a chatbot is good for here, what it isn’t, and prompts that work.

From the book: Appendix D, “The Chatbot Prompt Pack,” which has five more.

All the contract text in the book is posted as plain text so that you can paste it into an AI chatbot and ask it questions: the default contract, the Generous Contract, the No-Surprises Contract and the Children’s Contract. Here is what chatbots are good for and what they aren’t, with prompts that work.

What a chatbot can and can’t do for you

It’s good at explaining a clause in plain language. It’s good at comparing two versions of a term, and at generating questions to ask a lawyer. It’s also good at running through “what if” scenarios and at helping you put into words what you want.

It’s unreliable at stating your state’s current law, citing cases, and doing precise arithmetic on support formulas. Chatbots are known to invent statutes and court decisions that sound real. Anything it tells you about what the law is should be treated as a lead to check. Don’t rely on it as an answer.

It can’t tell you what a judge in your county would do, or make an agreement enforceable, or represent you.

Privacy. Assume that what you type may be stored. You can describe your situation accurately using round numbers and leaving out names. Don’t paste account numbers or Social Security numbers. If you’re in an unsafe relationship, don’t paste anything you wouldn’t want found, and consider who else can see your device and your chat history.

Prompts

Explain one article

I’m going to paste a reconstruction of the default marriage contract that applies to married couples in the United States who never signed a prenup. It comes from a book and isn’t a statute. Explain Article [4] to me in plain language, with two concrete examples. Then tell me what facts about my situation would change how it applies. Ask me those questions one at a time. [paste default.txt]

Find our surprises

Here is the default marriage contract. My spouse and I live in [state]. We’ve been married [N] years. One of us earns about [$X] and the other about [$Y]. We [do / do not] have children, [do / do not] own a home, and [one of us / neither of us] expects an inheritance. Based only on this document, list the five terms most likely to surprise a couple like us, and say why. Don’t state anything about [state]’s actual law unless you flag that I need to verify it. [paste default.txt]

Build my question list

My spouse and I have read the default marriage contract and want to change these terms: [list]. We live in [state]. Draft the ten questions that we should ask a family lawyer in a first consultation. Include questions about what’s enforceable in our state, what formalities apply, and what it’s likely to cost. Put the questions in order of importance.

Rehearse it

I want to raise the idea of reading our default marriage contract together with my [fiancé / spouse]. I’m worried that it will sound as if I expect the marriage to fail. Play my partner, reacting the way a reasonable but slightly hurt person might, and let me practice. After each of my replies, step out of character briefly and tell me how it landed.

A prompt to avoid

Don’t ask a chatbot to “write us a prenup for [state]” and then sign what comes back. The draft may be decent, and the drafts get better every few months, so this advice may not age well. Drafting quality isn’t what makes it risky today. A prenup holds up because each of you can show you were informed and independently advised, and as of this edition no court has accepted “the chatbot explained it to me” as either. Somebody will be the test case for that argument. It doesn’t need to be you, with your house at stake. There are narrower risks too. The draft may leave out formalities your state requires, and it will probably ignore the rule that retirement-plan waivers need their own form. Use the chatbot to understand the terms and to get ready for the lawyer. For now, the document you sign should come from lawyers, and each of you should have your own.
