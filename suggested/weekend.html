Ten Things to Do This Weekend

Ten small jobs, in order, from fifteen minutes to an afternoon. None of them requires a lawyer to start, and none requires imagining your marriage ending.

From the book: Chapter 36.

A book like this can leave you with a vague sense that you ought to Do Something. This page lists what to do. The jobs are in order of effort, and the first few are close to free. Do them together if you can.

1. Check every beneficiary form (15 minutes)

Log in to every retirement account and life insurance policy, including the ones from old jobs. Look at who’s actually named, and take screenshots. Make sure each has a backup beneficiary, and that no minor child is named directly. Show each other what you found. If you do nothing else in this book, do this one. (Chapter 10.) 1

The audit, as a checklist

2. Look up your state (20 minutes)

Go to your state’s page at marriagecontract.org and find out which version of each article you live under. Is your state community property or equitable distribution? Is it all-property? Does it set alimony by formula, by cap, or by shrug? Does fault count? Which necessaries rule applies? What’s the elective share? Note the date your state uses to close the marital pile. Most people are surprised by what they find. (Chapters 11 and 22.)

Find your state’s page

3. Read the term sheet separately, then compare marks (30 minutes)

Each of you reads the two-page contract in Chapter 1 and marks the articles that surprised you. The English version is fine for this. Then swap. Don’t debate yet. Just see where each other’s surprises were. (Chapter 2.)

The term sheet and the worksheet

4. Take the “Whose Is It?” quiz, separately (20 minutes)

Write down your answers before you compare. Every question where the two of you gave different answers marks an asset in your own house that you’ve been classifying differently, maybe for years. (Chapter 11.)

Take the quiz

5. Trade debt lists and pull your credit reports (30 minutes)

Each of you lists every debt with your name on it. Trade lists, then check them against your free credit reports. Couples nearly always find something one of them forgot, such as an old store card, a student loan, or a loan co-signed for a sibling. (Chapter 8.)

AnnualCreditReport.com

6. Find the paper (an hour)

If either of you has separate property that matters to you, find the statement closest to your wedding date, and do it now. Banks purge records after about seven years. You’ll need it for an inheritance, for savings from before the marriage, or for a house one of you already owned. While you’re in the files, look for a few other things. Find your last joint tax return and read page one. If you were married before, find your divorce decree, and make sure you have the final decree, which is a different document from the filing. If you’ve done IVF, dig out the clinic consent form and read its divorce-and-death section. Check whose name is on the pet’s microchip. (Chapters 5, 8, 12, and 18.)

Keeping “mine” mine

7. Write down two numbers and a date (15 minutes)

Do this privately. First, write down the percentage of everything you own that would seem fair to you if the two of you split up tomorrow. Second, for each year one of you has stepped back from paid work for the family, or might in the future, decide what that year is worth, as a share of the other’s income. Third, write the date on which you’d say your economic partnership began. Then show each other. If your percentages add up to 100, you agree on the biggest term in your contract and could write it down tonight. If they add up to 120, you’re like most couples. Either way, you’ve found something out. (Chapters 5, 14, and 15.)

8. Make the four documents (an afternoon, and a modest fee)

Each of you needs a will, a health care proxy, a durable power of attorney, and a short form naming who handles your funeral. If this is a second marriage, or if either of you has children from before, you need more than a simple will. Read Chapter 21 before you see the lawyer. If you aren’t married, these four documents are the only protection you have. (Chapters 6, 9, 20, and 21.)

9. Get the number (one hour with a lawyer)

Book a one-hour consultation with a family lawyer in your state and ask one question. If we divorced today, what’s the realistic range for property and support? You aren’t hiring anyone. You’re paying to learn what the default contract would actually do with your real assets and incomes, so that you can decide whether it’s what you want. Ask what the local judges tend to do, too. Expect a range. If the lawyer gives you one confident number, be skeptical. (Chapters 2 and 3.)

Finding a lawyer

10. Decide

After the first nine, you’ll be in one of three places.

The default suits you. That’s fine. Plenty of people read it and decide it suits them. Write one line saying so, put a date on it, and set a reminder to look again in five years or when you move states, whichever comes first. At that point the default is something you’ve chosen, which it wasn’t before.

You’d change two or three things. That list is your agenda for a lawyer. It will be a short and inexpensive meeting, because you’ve already done the part that usually takes longest, which is finding out what each of you wants. Appendix B may have language close to what you need.

You disagree about something important. It’s much better to learn that now. Chapter 32 has suggestions for how to talk about it, and a mediator can help you draft something together without anyone having to win. You’d have had this disagreement eventually. Now you can have it over a weekend, without lawyers billing by the hour.

The contracts in Appendix B

Serious for a Minute This chapter assumes two people who can sit down together safely and in good faith. If your partner controls the money, monitors you, or frightens you, don’t do these jobs with them. Do numbers 1, 2, 5, and 6 alone and quietly, and keep copies somewhere your partner can’t reach. Then talk to an advocate before you talk to your spouse: 1-800-799-7233 reaches the National Domestic Violence Hotline, and Appendix E lists other places to turn.
