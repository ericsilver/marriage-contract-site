Keeping “Mine” Mine

How separate property stays separate, and the household sentences that undo it.

From the book: Chapter 12.

Keeping “mine” mine, and the sentences that undo it

If you have separate property and want it to stay that way:

  • Keep the wedding-day statement. Save a statement for every account you own, dated as close to the wedding as you can get. It’s the most valuable piece of paper in this chapter. Banks generally purge records after about seven years, so get it now if you don’t already have it.
  • One account, one purpose. Inherited money goes into an account in your name alone. Nothing else ever goes into that account. That rules out a paycheck, a tax refund, and even twenty dollars.
  • Don’t add names. If a lender or a lawyer says you have to, ask what it will do to the property’s character in a divorce. Then decide knowing the answer.
  • If you use it for something joint, paper it. A signed paragraph written at the time is enough. Say whether it’s a loan or a gift.
  • Pay the taxes on separate income from the separate account. If your separate account’s tax bill is paid from marital funds, that starts a marital claim.

If your spouse has separate property and you’d like a share, these sentences will get you one. People say them every day, nearly always in good faith:

  • “Let’s just put it toward the mortgage.”
  • “It’s simpler if everything’s in one account.”
  • “The estate lawyer says both names should be on the deed.”
  • “We should use some of that to redo the kitchen. It’s an investment.”

Each of them is good household advice. Each of them also moves property from one spouse’s column to the marriage’s, and the move is permanent. We’re not suggesting anyone’s spouse is scheming. The contract treats ordinary generosity as a legal transfer and never tells either of you that it’s doing so.

The fair version is to decide on purpose. Plenty of couples want the inheritance to become ours. Appendix B’s Generous Contract makes that the rule for everything. Other couples want a strict wall, and Appendix B’s No-Surprises Contract builds one, with a door in it for the family home. Both arrangements work. The arrangement that produces lawsuits is one where each spouse privately assumed something different.
