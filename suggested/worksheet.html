Reading it together

A worksheet for the contract you already have

This is the exercise from Chapters 2 and 36 of the book, on one sheet. Print it, or copy the table. It takes about an hour, it needs no lawyer, and nothing on it commits either of you to anything.

How to use it

  1. Read the summaries separately. Each of you checks your own column for every article that surprised you. Don’t compare yet.
  2. Compare checks. Don’t debate. Just notice where each of you checked. You’ll probably find you didn’t check the same articles.
  3. Choose an article and read the chapter on it. Begin with one that affects every couple: debts (5), death (9), or the federal riders (10). The chapter on beneficiary forms (Chapter 10) tends to send both of you off to check your accounts online.
  4. Decide about that one article. Keep it, change it, or ask a lawyer. “Keep” is a fine answer. Write it down anyway.
  5. Repeat until you’re bored. Whatever is left under “change” and “ask” is your agenda for a one-hour meeting with a family lawyer in your state.

For the lawyer

Take the sheet and three questions to a one-hour consultation:

  1. Given our assets and incomes and this state’s law, what would the default do to us now? Ask for a range, not a single figure.
  2. Would this state refuse to enforce anything we want?
  3. What does an agreement need here in order to hold up: disclosure, timing, separate lawyers, notarization?

Before you go, look up your state’s Luck of the Draw score and its page. The full texts of the default contract and three alternatives are on the contracts page, licensed so you can copy and change them, and a comparison page lays out three versions of each term for each of you to rank.

This worksheet and its summaries describe how the law generally works. They aren’t legal advice, and no lawyer has reviewed them. The summaries restate the term sheet in Chapter 1 of the book.
