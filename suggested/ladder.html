The Ladder

The routes through a divorce, from cheapest to most expensive, and what to do if you can’t afford a lawyer.

From the book: Chapter 28, “What It Costs”.

Seven routes, cheapest first

You can get divorced in at least seven ways. They’re listed below from cheapest to most expensive. The right one is the lowest rung that fits your situation.

RouteHow it worksFits when
Do it yourselvesYou agree on everything, fill out the court’s forms, and file. Courts have self-help centers, and online services will prepare the forms for a few hundred dollars.Short marriage, no children, little property, no pension
Simplified procedureMany states have a fast track for couples who qualify: short marriage, no children, low assets, full agreement.You meet your state’s checklist
MediationOne neutral mediator helps the two of you reach terms. Each of you may have a lawyer review the result.You can be in a room together and both know the finances
Collaborative divorceEach has a lawyer, and everyone signs a pledge not to go to court (Chapter 26). Often includes a shared financial neutral.Good faith on both sides, real complexity
Lawyer-negotiated settlementEach hires a lawyer, the lawyers exchange disclosures and proposals, and the case settles before trial. This is the most common route.Most contested cases
Arbitration or private judgeYou hire the decision-maker (Chapter 3). Faster and private. Money issues only in most states.You can’t agree but want speed and privacy
TrialChapter 3.Nothing else has worked, or the other side is hiding things or is dangerous

Two cautions about the bottom rungs. A do-it-yourself divorce is a false economy if there’s a pension, a business, or a house with real equity. A mistake in dividing any of those costs far more than the lawyer you didn’t hire, and Article 11.5 makes property settlements final. Mediation is also the wrong choice where there has been abuse or where one spouse doesn’t know what the couple owns. Chapter 25 covers the first situation and Chapter 27 the second.

Strategies

Buying Less Lawyer

Hire one for a single task. Most states now permit limited-scope representation, sometimes called unbundled services. You handle your own case, and you pay a lawyer for one defined job. That could be reviewing the settlement before you sign, coaching you for a hearing, drafting the order that divides the pension, or appearing at the one hearing that matters. Spending $1,500 to have a specialist read a settlement you negotiated yourselves is very good value.

Ask about flat fees. A growing number of family lawyers will quote a fixed price for an uncontested divorce, a prenup, or a specific motion. A flat fee gives the lawyer a reason to work efficiently, and it spares you from worrying about the meter.

Settle the small things yourselves. Divide the furniture, the dishes, and the air fryer at the kitchen table, using Chapter 18’s procedure if it helps. Bring the lawyers only the questions that are worth $450 an hour.

Use a mediator for the middle, and lawyers only at the ends. One mediator at $300 an hour is cheaper than two lawyers at $450 each, and a mediator’s role is to help the two of you agree.

Set a budget and say it out loud. Tell your lawyer at the first meeting what you can spend. A good one will tell you what that buys. Chapter 26 explained why you should write that number down.

The cheapest route isn’t on the ladder. It’s agreeing to the terms years before you need them, which is the subject of Part 9.

If you can’t afford a lawyer at all

Chapter 3 noted that in most family court cases, at least one side has no lawyer. If that’s going to be you, you have more options than you may think.

Court self-help centers. Most courthouses have one. The staff can’t give legal advice, but they can explain the forms, the deadlines, and the procedure, and the service is free.

Legal aid. Every state has legal aid organizations for people with low incomes. They can’t take every case, and they give priority to cases involving domestic violence and children. Apply even if you aren’t sure you qualify.

Law school clinics. Many law schools run family law clinics where supervised students handle real cases at no charge.

Bar association referral and modest-means programs. Many state and county bars keep lists of lawyers who take cases at reduced rates for people who earn too much for legal aid and too little for $270 an hour.

Fee waivers. If you can’t afford the filing fee, ask the clerk for the waiver form.

Limited scope. Even a single paid hour with a family lawyer, spent reviewing a settlement before you sign it, is worth more than any other hour of help you could buy.

A fee award. If your spouse has money and you don’t, see the section above. The court can order your spouse to pay for your lawyer, and a lawyer’s first consultation can tell you whether that’s realistic.

If you’re in danger, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 before any of these. Advocates there can connect you to lawyers who handle protective orders at no cost.
