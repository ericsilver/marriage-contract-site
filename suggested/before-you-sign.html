Before You Sign

The order that works, a checklist for two, and what to ask if someone hands you a draft.

From the book: Chapters 29, 30 and 32.

The order of operations

Couples who come through this with their good humor intact tend to follow the same sequence.

  1. Start with each other, and leave the lawyers out. Read the term sheet, take the quiz, and mark up the table. You’re done when you have one page you both recognize.
  2. Get the number. One of you, or both, spends an hour with a local family lawyer to learn what the default would do today. The answer is often “about what you’d have written anyway,” and in that case you can stop.
  3. If you’re going ahead, choose the mediated route unless there’s a reason not to.
  4. Bring in the second lawyer early. A lawyer who’s first called the week before signing can only say “don’t sign this.”
  5. Finish three months before the wedding. If it’s a postnup, finish in a calm season.
  6. Sign the retirement-plan forms in the first week of the marriage (Chapter 10).
  7. Put the five-year review on a calendar that the two of you share.

When it goes wrong, the order has usually been reversed. One side’s lawyer wrote the first draft. It arrived by surprise, and the conversation came last.

A checklist for two

If the earlier chapters left you with two or three things you’d change, this is the procedure that makes the change hold.

Only the second item requires either of you to say anything awkward, and you’ve probably already said it. The ten-minute exercise at the end of Chapter 8 covered most of the same ground.

If you’re handed one of these

Suppose someone has given you a draft and asked you to sign it. Here are the three questions that matter most.

What happens to me if I stop working? Find the support section. If it’s a flat waiver with no exception for raising children, illness, or disability, it’s the most dangerous clause in the document. Ask for the trigger described above. A partner who intends to treat you fairly will agree to it, because it costs nothing unless the situation it covers comes up.

What counts as “separate,” and what does its growth count as? If everything your partner owns stays separate, and so does everything it earns and everything it grows into, a twenty-year marriage can end with no marital property at all. Ask for a share of the growth, even a modest one that rises over time.

What happens if my spouse dies? If you’re waiving the elective share, ask what replaces it. Life insurance, a right to stay in the home, and a trust are the usual answers. “Nothing” isn’t one you should accept.

Get your own lawyer, one you’ve chosen. If you’re told there isn’t time or money for that, Chapter 29 described how courts treat that situation. Knowing that, you can decide whether to believe it.
