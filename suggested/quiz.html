Whose Is It?

Twelve questions about who owns what if a couple without a prenup divorces. Most people miss several.

From the book: Chapter 11, “Whose Is It?”.

In each question, assume a married couple with no prenup, and ask who owns the thing if they divorce. 1

  1. 1. Her paycheck, deposited for ten years into an account in her name only.

    The answer

    Ours, in every state. Earnings during the marriage belong to the marriage, and it makes no difference whose name is on the account. Chapter 7 explained that a name on an account decides who controls the money while you’re married. It doesn’t decide who gets it at divorce. This is the rule people most often get wrong.

  2. 2. The $80,000 that was in his 401(k) on the wedding day.

    The answer

    His. So is the investment growth on that $80,000, in most states. Everything he contributed after the wedding is ours, along with its growth. Someone will have to separate the two streams twenty years later, and the person who kept the wedding-day statement wins. In Pennsylvania and a few other states, even the growth on the premarital balance counts as ours.

  3. 3. The $200,000 she inherited from her mother last year, kept in her own account.

    The answer

    Hers, in most states. Gifts and inheritances to one spouse are separate property even when they arrive in the middle of the marriage. The exception is the group of “all-property” states, which includes Massachusetts and Connecticut. A judge there can reach anything either spouse owns. And even in the other states it stops being hers if she deposits it in the joint account, which is the subject of Chapter 12.

  4. 4. The house he bought two years before the wedding. The mortgage has been paid from his salary ever since.

    The answer

    Part his, part ours, and the split depends on your state. His salary during the marriage was marital money, so marital money has been buying equity in a separate house. California gives the community a proportional share of the house, including a share of its appreciation. Texas takes the opposite view. It says the house is his because it was his on the day he got it, and the community can only ask to be reimbursed for the principal it paid. Most states fall somewhere in between. This one question accounts for a large share of all the legal fees spent on property.

  5. 5. Her engagement ring.

    The answer

    Hers. It was a gift made before the marriage, and the gift became final when the wedding took place. Chapter 18 covers what happens when the wedding never takes place.

  6. 6. The diamond necklace he gave her for their tenth anniversary.

    The answer

    Probably ours. In many states a gift from one spouse to the other, bought with marital money, is still marital property. The marriage bought itself a necklace. California has a statute saying that a gift of jewelry that is “substantial in value” stays community property unless there’s a written document. Almost nobody expects this answer.

  7. 7. The wedding gifts.

    The answer

    Ours, generally. The exception is a gift that was plainly meant for one of you. Your grandmother’s china came from your grandmother, and courts tend to see it that way.

  8. 8. The winning lottery ticket he bought with two dollars from his wallet.

    The answer

    Ours. The ticket was bought during the marriage, so the winnings are marital. People who have tried to hide a winning ticket have done very badly, as Chapter 14 shows. What happens if he buys it after moving out depends on the date your state uses to close the marital pile, which is what the rest of this chapter is about.

  9. 9. Her $300,000 settlement from a car accident.

    The answer

    It gets taken apart, in most states. The part that pays for her pain and suffering is hers, because nothing is more separate than your own body. The part that replaces wages she would have earned during the marriage is ours. So is the part that repays medical bills the marriage covered. A minority of states don’t divide it this way and treat the whole settlement as marital.

  10. 10. The business he started in year three. She has never set foot in it.

    The answer

    Ours. He built it with his labor during the marriage, and his labor during the marriage belongs to the marriage. Her not having worked there doesn’t matter. He’ll usually keep the business and pay her for her share, which means somebody has to decide what it’s worth (Chapter 13).

  11. 11. Stock options granted to her in year eight, which vest two years after the divorce.

    The answer

    Partly ours. Courts use a time formula. They take the number of months from the grant to the divorce and divide by the number of months from the grant to vesting. That fraction of the options is marital. The reasoning is that the options were partly pay for work she did while married, and partly an incentive to stay on afterward.

  12. 12. His medical degree, earned while she worked two jobs to support them.

    The answer

    His, and it isn’t property at all. Nearly every state says a degree or a license can’t be divided. For thirty years New York was the famous exception (Chapter 13). Some states will order him to reimburse her for what she paid toward it. Others just count it as a reason to give her more of everything else.

12 to go.

If you scored under eight, you’re in good company. Questions 1, 4, 6, and 10 catch most people, including plenty who are sure they know how this works.

Take it together

Write down your answers before you compare. Every question where the two of you gave different answers marks an asset in your own house that you’ve been classifying differently, maybe for years.
