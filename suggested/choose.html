Three versions of each term

Rank them separately, then compare

For each term below there are three versions: what your state gives you if you sign nothing, the Generous Contract’s version, and the No-Surprises Contract’s version. Each of you ranks the three, 1 for the one you’d most want. Do it separately, on paper or here, without looking at the other’s answers. Then compare. The terms where your first choices differ are your agenda, and the ones where they match are already settled.

These are plain-English summaries. The full texts are on the contracts page, free to copy. This isn’t legal advice, no lawyer has looked it over, and any agreement you sign needs a separate lawyer for each of you in your state. Nothing gets saved or sent: your choices stay on this page in your browser.

What either of you earns during the marriage

What either of you earns while you’re married belongs to you both, whoever’s name is on it. Nine states treat that as half each from the start, though only three must split it evenly at a divorce. In the others it means what a judge considers fair, and a few states let the judge reach premarital and inherited property as well.

It’s all ours: what we each brought, and whatever we inherit. Schedule C lists the only exceptions. No one ever has to hunt for old bank statements.

Whatever either of us earns during the marriage is split evenly, including retirement contributions, stock grants, and business growth.

What each of you brought in, and inheritances

What you owned going in stays yours, along with anything you’re given or inherit, provided you keep it fully separate and can document that years from now. Mixing it in, putting it in joint names, or spending marital money on it turns some or all of it into shared property.

It’s all ours: what we each brought, and whatever we inherit. Schedule C lists the only exceptions. No one ever has to hunt for old bank statements.

Keep what you brought in your own name and it stays yours, and the same goes for inheritances. Move it to a joint account and it’s shared. One exception: separate money used to buy the family home comes back to you dollar for dollar, with no growth.

Debts

Debts are divided the way assets are, but that division doesn’t bind the lender. If your ex is ordered to pay the joint card and doesn’t, the lender can still collect from you. Your spouse’s hospital bills may be yours as well.

We share every debt, apart from what’s spent on an affair or gambling.

We share family debts. Debts from before the marriage stay with whoever brought them. A student loan taken out while married becomes shared once five years have passed.

Support after a divorce

After a divorce, the spouse with more may owe the spouse with less. Most states can’t tell you how much or for how long until a judge decides. A few use a formula, a few limit it deliberately, and in a handful an affair can cost you your alimony.

The higher earner pays 35% of the difference in income, for as long as the marriage lasted or, after a marriage of 15 years or more, until retirement age. Fault doesn’t reduce it.

The higher earner pays 30% of the difference in income. Length depends on the marriage: no support under three years, half the marriage’s length up to twenty, and past twenty, until the payer’s retirement age.

Stepping back from work for the family

No separate credit. A judge may take it into account in setting support, or may not.

The spouse who stepped back from a career gets retraining money from the shared property before we divide it.

Each year one of us stays home or works part-time for the family, that spouse earns 15% of the other’s pay for the year, payable if we split. The rate is set in advance, so there’s nothing to fight about later.

The family home

Part of the division, like everything else. The judge decides who keeps it, if anyone.

We divide everything in half. The lower earner or main caregiver can keep the house, with as long as five years to pay any difference.

Either of us can buy out the other’s share of the house, priced by appraisal. We sell it if neither does, or if we both want it.

If one of you dies

If there’s no will, your spouse takes a big portion of the estate. If a will leaves your spouse out, your spouse can set it aside and claim a set minimum. Georgia does it differently.

Almost everything goes to the surviving spouse. Our beneficiary forms name each other, and we keep substantial life insurance while our kids are young.

The usual rules at death still apply. Every five years we exchange copies of our beneficiary forms, which heads off most of the disasters.

If it ends: how disagreements get decided

You get one judge and no jury, and that judge has so much latitude that appeals rarely succeed. So most couples settle, which means bargaining over a forecast of what that judge would do. You each pay your own lawyer unless the judge orders otherwise.

Mediation comes first. Joint funds pay both lawyers, which keeps either of us from outspending the other.

Thirty days’ notice before anyone files. Mediation comes next. If that fails, a private arbitrator settles the money questions. Turn down a written offer and fail to beat it by the agreed margin, and you pay your spouse’s legal fees.

Which state’s rules apply

The state you end up in writes the rules, as they stand on the day the marriage ends. A move can change them, and either of you can move.

Not addressed, so the rules of the state you live in when it ends.

We choose one state’s rules today, and they travel with us. Most courts will respect that, but not every court will.

Reviewing the deal

Your state’s lawmakers and courts can change the deal at any time, without your consent.

We go back over it every five years and after any major life event.

We go back through it every five years and after any major change. Amendments are done properly, with signatures and lawyers.
