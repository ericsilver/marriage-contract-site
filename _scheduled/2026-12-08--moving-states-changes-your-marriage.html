Moving to another state changes your marriage contract

Eric Silver · December 8, 2026

The rules on marital property, spousal support and prenups differ from one state to the next. When a couple moves, the set of rules that would apply to them if the marriage ended can change with the address. No state sends a notice when that happens.

The states really are different

A few examples show the spread. California, Texas, Washington and Louisiana are community property states. New York, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania and Illinois divide property under equitable distribution. Even among the community property states, the details differ. Texas lets its courts divide community property on a “just and right” basis, and it caps spousal support in both amount and length.

So the same couple, with the same savings and the same history, can face a different set of terms depending on where they happen to be living when the question comes up. A move changes more than the property tax bill.

Some of the old rules come along

A move doesn’t wipe the slate clean. Property generally keeps the character it had in the state where it was acquired. Money earned while a couple lived in California is still community property after they move to New York. A couple who spend some years in a community property state and then leave have taken part of that state’s law with them, whether or not anyone in the new state notices.

The prenup that chose its own state

A prenup can do something a move can’t. It can name the state whose law will govern it. The case that shows what that clause can be worth is DeLorean v. DeLorean, decided by a New Jersey court in 1986.

John DeLorean married Cristina in 1973. On May 8 of that year, only a few hours before the wedding, he gave her a premarital agreement to sign. She was 23. He was 25 years older. The agreement kept each spouse’s property separate. Its description of his wealth was short: a California farm, some Montana land, and part ownership of a baseball club. The lawyer who reviewed it for her had been chosen by her husband, and even that lawyer told her not to sign it. She signed it anyway.

When the marriage ended, the divorce was heard in New Jersey. The court said the assets at stake could exceed $20 million. The judge also wrote that the financial disclosure in the agreement “fell far short” of what New Jersey expected.

Under New Jersey’s standards, the agreement was in trouble. But the contract said California law governed it, and the court followed the contract. California’s standard at the time was less demanding about disclosure. Applying California law, the court enforced the agreement.

A single clause took a New Jersey divorce and decided it under California’s rules. For a couple who expect to move, that is the useful lesson. A choice-of-law clause is a term the two of you can settle in advance. Where you will be living in twenty years is not.

Military families

Military couples have an extra layer. Federal law lets a servicemember keep a legal residence for tax purposes wherever the military posts them. The state on the orders and the state of legal residence need not be the same. For a military couple, it’s worth knowing which is which before anyone files anything.

Why people don’t check

Psychologists David Schkade and Daniel Kahneman surveyed students in the Midwest and in California about their own happiness, and about how happy a similar student would be in the other region. Each group predicted that the Californians would come out ahead. In fact the two groups reported about the same level of happiness. The students had fixed on climate, the difference easiest to picture. The researchers called this a focusing illusion.

Choosing a state to live in invites the same mistake. The job and the house get the attention. How the new state would treat an inheritance in a divorce, or how its courts set support, rarely comes up.

What you can do

Before or soon after a move, find out how your new state divides property, how it sets support, what it guarantees a surviving spouse and how it treats a spouse’s debts. Your state’s page, at the state pages, gets more detail as each one is checked.

If you have a prenup or a postnup, look for a clause naming the state whose law applies. If there isn’t one, ask a lawyer in the new state whether it should, and whether any of its terms could run into trouble there.

If you are leaving a community property state, hold what you earned there in separate accounts, and save the account statements from moving day. You may need to show later what that money was.
