Your divorce decree says your ex pays the credit card. The bank never agreed.

Eric Silver · December 1, 2026

A divorce court can decide which of you pays each marital debt. It can’t change who owes the lender. If your name is on a joint credit card and the decree assigns that card to your ex, the bank can still collect from you. The allocation binds the two of you and nobody else.

What the decree does

At a divorce, the judge or the settlement divides the debts along with the assets. One spouse keeps the car and its loan. The other keeps a card balance. Everyone signs, and both people go home believing the question is closed.

The card issuer was never part of that case. Its agreement is with the people who signed the account, and a family court has no power to rewrite it. So when an ex who was assigned a joint card stops paying, the missed payments show up on your credit report as well as theirs. Collectors will phone you as well.

The decree does give you one thing. If you end up paying a debt the court assigned to your ex, you can go back to court and ask your ex to reimburse you. That right is only as good as your ex’s willingness and ability to pay. Someone who has just stopped paying the card is not a promising source of reimbursement.

The same logic runs through the basic rule on marital debt. In most states, you aren’t liable for your spouse’s debts simply because you’re married. You are liable for anything you signed, co-signed or guaranteed. A divorce doesn’t erase your signature.

Getting your name off the debt

The only reliable protection is to stop being a borrower. A decree can’t do that for you. The steps are plain, if tedious:

  • Pay off joint credit cards and close them.
  • The spouse keeping the car should take out a new loan for it alone.
  • Sell the house, or have the spouse who keeps it refinance the mortgage. A promise to pay a mortgage that still carries both names protects nobody.
  • For anything that can’t be settled before the divorce is final, get security you could actually collect on.

The tax bill follows the same rule

The IRS isn’t bound by your decree either. Every person who signs a joint federal return is liable for the entire tax on it. That includes tax on income the other spouse hid or reported wrongly. The IRS can collect it after the divorce, regardless of how the decree divides the couple’s obligations.

The tax code does offer a way out, called innocent spouse relief. To qualify, a spouse must prove three things: no knowledge of the understatement, no reason to have known of it, and that making them pay would be inequitable. That test is harder to meet than it sounds.

Kathryn Cheshire’s case shows how hard. Her husband, David, received $229,924 in retirement distributions. He told her an accountant had advised him that most of it wasn’t taxable since the couple had put it toward paying off their mortgage. No accountant had said anything of the kind. The joint return he prepared reported only a small part of the distributions as income. Kathryn asked him about it and accepted his answer. She signed the return on March 14, 1993.

The couple separated that July and divorced seventeen months later. When the IRS pursued the unpaid tax, Kathryn asked for innocent spouse relief. The Fifth Circuit held her liable for it. The courts asked whether she had known about the income, not whether she had known the return was wrong. She knew about the distributions and roughly what they came to. Believing her husband’s account of the tax law didn’t qualify her. She did win relief from part of an accuracy penalty. She was not relieved of the tax.

Cheshire is a hard result, and it follows directly from the rule. Signing a joint return makes the whole bill yours, and a later divorce doesn’t send any of it back.

What you can do

Before a divorce is final, list every debt that carries your name. Your free credit reports are the place to check. For each joint account, decide how your name comes off it: payoff, closure, refinance or sale. Treat a decree that only assigns the debt as unfinished business.

For taxes, read a joint return before you sign it, and ask about any figure that surprises you. If your spouse’s explanations are vague, filing separately is an option. A separate return keeps your spouse’s understatement from becoming yours.
