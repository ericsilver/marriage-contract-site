Can you be forced to testify against your spouse?

Eric Silver · November 24, 2026

In a criminal case, a prosecutor generally can’t compel you to take the stand against your husband or wife, though the details vary between federal court and the states. That protection is one of the benefits that come with marriage. In federal court, though, it belongs to you as the witness. It does not belong to your spouse. If you decide to testify, your spouse has no way to stop you.

That last part surprises people, who tend to picture “spousal privilege” as a shield the defendant raises. The law actually has two privileges, and they follow different rules.

Two privileges

The first is the testimonial privilege. It concerns the act of testifying at all: generally, a spouse can’t be made to give evidence against the other in a criminal prosecution. It exists only while the marriage exists. Once the divorce is final, it’s gone.

The second is the communications privilege. It concerns what the two of you told each other in confidence during the marriage. Generally either spouse can claim it, and unlike the testimonial privilege, it outlives a divorce. So an ex-spouse who can now be called as a witness may still be unable to repeat a private conversation from the years you were married.

Keeping the two apart answers most questions. Whether you can refuse to testify is one question. Whether a particular conversation is protected is another. A divorce changes the answer to the first and leaves the second alone.

The Supreme Court’s answer: the witness decides

The leading federal case is Trammel v. United States, a 1980 Supreme Court decision. The Court held that the testimonial privilege belongs to the witness-spouse alone. Nobody can force the witness to testify. Nobody can forbid it either.

The practical effect is that the defendant has no veto. A spouse who chooses to cooperate with prosecutors may do so, and the marriage is no obstacle. A spouse who refuses can’t be compelled. The decision rests with the person in the witness box, whatever the person at the defense table would prefer.

This matters most if you are the witness. The privilege is yours to use or to give up. Your spouse can’t invoke it on your behalf, and your spouse’s lawyer works for your spouse.

Marrying the witness

Crime fiction has a favorite shortcut. A defendant proposes to the prosecution’s most important witness. They marry before the trial begins. The new spouse then declines to testify, and the case falls apart.

Real courtrooms are less obliging. The tactic succeeds less often than the plot suggests, partly because courts carve out exceptions for sham marriages. The Supreme Court dealt with sham marriages in Lutwak v. United States, decided in 1953.

A real marriage is different. If the wedding is genuine and the witness is now genuinely a spouse, the privilege applies. Even then, Trammel shapes the outcome in federal court. The privilege belongs to the witness, so the defendant’s plan depends on the new spouse continuing to say no. It also depends on the marriage lasting, since the testimonial privilege ends with it. A defendant who marries the witness is betting on the relationship holding up at least until the verdict.

The whole arrangement is an odd corner of family law. Most of the benefits of marriage involve taxes, health plans, hospitals and inheritance. This one was built for the courtroom, and the people most interested in it tend to be under indictment.

What you can do

If you are asked to testify in a case against your spouse, start by recognizing that the choice is yours, at least in federal court. Get advice from a lawyer who represents you. Don’t rely on your spouse’s lawyer to explain your options, because that lawyer’s duty runs to your spouse.

Sort out which privilege is in play. If the question is whether you have to appear and testify, that’s the testimonial privilege, and it depends on whether you’re still married. If the question is whether a specific private conversation can be used, that’s the communications privilege, and it survives divorce.
