How an inheritance quietly becomes marital property

Eric Silver · October 20, 2026

An inheritance usually starts out as the separate property of the spouse who receives it. Texas, for example, defines separate property to include anything a spouse acquires by gift, devise, or descent. Keeping it separate is the harder part. Depending on your state, depositing it in a joint account, putting it into a jointly titled house, or failing to keep records can turn some or all of it into property the two of you share.

A sensible decision, eleven years later

Take an example. You inherit $180,000 from your mother. You and your spouse still owe $175,000 on a house titled in both your names. Paying off the mortgage saves interest and relieves the whole household, so you write the check.

You divorce eleven years later. By then the house is worth $600,000, with nothing owed on it.

How much of that value counts as your mother’s money depends on the state. In California, property held in joint form is presumed to be community property. Changing the character of property there requires an express declaration. California does give back a separate contribution that can be traced to the asset, but the refund comes without interest. Other states have their own rules for jointly titled property and for separate money spent on it.

Many people think the family home should belong to the family, however it was paid for. Others are surprised that one check can decide a property division a decade later. A court doesn’t ask which view you hold. It looks at the title and the records.

The candy heir who couldn’t show his work

Records matter as much as title. California’s rule on them comes from a divorce in a chocolate-making family.

Laurance See ran See’s Candies, the company his family had started. He brought substantial wealth into his marriage. He also earned a large salary during it, and in California that salary was community property.

Over a marriage of twenty-one years, his separate income and his community salary went into the same accounts, and the family’s living costs were paid from the combined funds. Elizabeth filed for divorce, and Laurance argued that almost all that remained belonged to him alone.

The argument was arithmetic. He totaled the community income across the entire marriage, then totaled the family’s living expenses over the same years. Expenses were larger. The community, he said, had spent every dollar it ever earned, so whatever remained must have come from his separate funds.

In 1966 the California Supreme Court rejected that approach. A spouse can’t wait until the marriage is over and compare lifetime totals. Someone who claims that an asset was bought with separate money has to show that separate money was available and used when the asset was bought. The court put that burden on the spouse who mixed the funds. In its words, a spouse who commingles “assumes the burden of keeping records adequate to establish the balance of community income and expenditures at the time an asset is acquired.” Laurance had not kept such records, and he lost.

Two ledgers in one house

Part of the difficulty is psychological. In a well-known experiment, students who had just been given a coffee mug asked roughly twice as much to part with it as other students would pay to buy one. That is the endowment effect. An inheritance carries a similar attachment. The heir sees their parent’s money, now sitting inside the house. The other spouse sees the house.

The same gap shows up in public. In November 2024 a husband asked Reddit for help explaining to his wife that his inheritance was “mine, not ‘ours’,” and that the spending decisions would be his. Moneywise and Yahoo Finance reported that about 18,000 people upvoted the post and 5,000 commented. It is a self-report, and none of its facts have been verified. If he lives in Texas, the inheritance itself would be his separate property, but the interest and dividends it earns are community property unless the two of them sign an agreement making that income separate.

What you can do

If you want an inheritance to stay yours:

  • Keep it in an account that is in your name only, and deposit nothing else there.
  • Keep every statement for that account.
  • If any of it goes toward something joint, put a short note in writing the same day, signed by both of you, stating whether it’s a loan to the household or a gift to it.
  • If you live in Texas and want the income to stay separate too, that takes a signed agreement.

If you want it to become shared, that is a legitimate choice, and it’s better made on purpose than by accident. The alternatives on the contracts page include one that treats inheritances as shared and one that keeps them separate as long as they stay in your own name.
