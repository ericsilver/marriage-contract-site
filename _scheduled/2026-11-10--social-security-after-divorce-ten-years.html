Divorced after ten years? The Social Security rule most people miss

Eric SilverNovember 10, 2026

If your marriage lasted ten years, you can draw Social Security on your former spouse’s earnings record after the divorce. If it lasted a little less, you can’t. The ten years are counted up to the day the divorce becomes final. The day one of you moved out doesn’t count.

What marriage gives you, and what survives divorce

While a marriage lasts, a spouse can receive up to half of the other spouse’s benefit, whether or not that spouse built up a record of their own. A widow or widower can move up to the deceased spouse’s full benefit. Nearly all of that disappears when the marriage ends. The main exception is the ten-year rule, which lets a divorced spouse keep a claim on the former spouse’s record.

The rights arrive on a schedule, and federal law sets the dates:

  • Nine months: the Social Security survivor benefit becomes available if a spouse dies. An accidental death is an exception to the waiting period.
  • One year: the spousal retirement benefit.
  • Ten years: a benefit on an ex-spouse’s record, even after divorce.

Ten years matters elsewhere in federal law too. Military retired pay can be divided by a court no matter how long the marriage lasted. The military pay office, however, sends the former spouse a check directly only when the marriage overlapped at least ten years of service. Otherwise the former spouse has to collect from the retiree each month.

A worked example

Take a couple who married in the spring and have been separated for most of their ninth year. The house is sold, the accounts are split, and both want the paperwork finished. One spouse earned far more over the marriage. The other stepped back from paid work for several years and has a thin earnings record.

If the divorce becomes final a few weeks before the tenth anniversary, the lower earner has no claim on the higher earner’s record. If it becomes final a few weeks after, the claim exists. Nothing else about the two divorces has to differ. The same decree, dated on the other side of the anniversary, produces a different result. The separation, the moving out, and the years of living apart before the decree do not move the date. Only the decree does.

It costs the other spouse nothing

The divorced spouse’s benefit doesn’t come out of the worker’s check. The worker’s own benefit stays the same whether a former spouse claims or not. The law also sets no limit on how many former spouses can claim on one record, and no former spouse’s benefit reduces another’s. A worker married four times, each marriage lasting ten years, could have four former spouses and a current spouse all drawing on the same record while the worker’s own payment stays unchanged.

That is unusual in a divorce. For most issues, what one spouse gains the other loses. Here the higher earner gives up nothing if the decree is dated after the anniversary. Both spouses can reasonably want the same timing.

Remarriage stops it

The benefit on a former spouse’s record is available only while you remain unmarried. A new marriage ends it. Anyone drawing on an ex-spouse’s record should count that cost before setting a wedding date.

What to do

  • Find the date of the marriage. Check it against the date the divorce is likely to become final. If the two fall on either side of a tenth anniversary, tell your lawyer.
  • Raise the timing directly with the other side. Because the benefit costs the higher earner nothing, a short delay in finalizing is an easy thing to agree to.
  • If there’s a military pension, check how many years of the marriage overlapped service. The ten-year mark decides whether the pay office will send payments directly.
  • If you’re already divorced after ten years or more, ask the Social Security Administration about a benefit on your former spouse’s record. Check again before you remarry.

Sources

  • 42 U.S.C. §§ 402(b), (c), (e), (f); 403(a)(3)(C); 416(c), (d), (g).
  • 10 U.S.C. § 1408(d)(2).
