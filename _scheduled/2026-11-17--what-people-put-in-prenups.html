What people actually put in prenups (it isn't always a wall)

Eric Silver · November 17, 2026

A prenup is a container, not a particular deal. It can keep each spouse’s money apart. It can pool everything, including what each person brought in. It can promise the spouse who earns less more than a court would ever order. The version most people picture is the first one: a document one side’s lawyer uses to protect one side’s money.

More couples are signing them. In a Harris Poll conducted for The New Yorker in May 2022, 15 percent of married or engaged Americans said they had signed a prenup. The comparable figure for 2010 was 3 percent.

Two couples, as they described themselves

In 2022 a woman described her situation in a Reddit post that Newsweek later reported. It is a self-report, and none of it has been verified. By her account, her husband’s relatives had spent years accusing her of marrying him for his money. Her own wealth, from selling a software company, ran to eight figures; his trust fund ran to seven. The 40-page prenup was something she had asked for. After an uncle claimed she had pushed her husband into buying an apartment that she had in fact paid for herself, she emailed the agreement to the family, with most of it redacted.

The relatives had assumed a prenup is something a wealthy man’s family presses on his bride. The agreement in this story protected the wife. A prenup protects whichever spouse has more to protect, and nothing about it requires that to be the husband.

A second self-report, from a 29-year-old man on Reddit in 2026 and also reported by Newsweek, shows a different kind of term: a payment to the spouse with less. He said the two of them had agreed that the prenup would pay her a six-figure amount for every biological child, to compensate her for pregnancy and childbirth. His parents objected that she was exploiting him, and they threatened to stay away from the wedding. As with the first story, none of it has been verified.

What the sections cover

Each section of a typical prenup replaces one of the rules the state would otherwise apply: what stays separate, how shared property is divided, who keeps the house or a business, who owes which debts, whether anyone pays support after a divorce, and what each spouse inherits. For each one, a couple can choose to keep things apart, split them evenly, or share more than the law requires. Some terms are built to change over time. A sunset ends part of the agreement after a set number of years. An escalator gives the spouse with less a better deal the longer the marriage lasts.

Where the first draft comes from

The terms a couple ends up with depend heavily on who writes the first draft. Research on anchoring, the tendency of a first number to pull later judgments toward it, suggests the starting document shapes the finished one. Few prenups are drafted from nothing. A lawyer adapts the firm’s form, and those forms tend to be written for the client who usually comes in asking for one, the spouse with more money. Couples who draft their own term sheet first, however rough, start from what both of them want.

The reported clauses

Celebrity prenups are private, and what is known about them comes from entertainment reporting rather than court files. As reported, an Oscar-winning actor agreed to pay his actress wife about $2.8 million for each year of marriage, plus $5 million if he were unfaithful. As reported, a country singer is paid about $600,000 for each year of his marriage to a movie star, a payment that stops if he returns to drugs. As reported, one film star’s third wife was to receive $3 million for each year of marriage for up to eleven years, and half of everything after that. They divorced about five and a half years in.

None of these can be checked against a record. Taken as reported, they share something. They are guaranteed sums or escalators, and those are terms that favor the spouse with less.

Three other starting points

The book’s appendix contains three complete alternatives, each designed around one purpose, and they are posted on the contracts page. The numbers in them are in brackets because they are placeholders for a couple to set; none is a recommendation.

  • The Generous Contract shares everything, including inheritances, except items the couple lists. Support is a share of the income gap (the draft’s placeholder is 35 percent), and if the law at divorce would give the lower earner more, the law wins.
  • The No-Surprises Contract keeps separate property separate unless it goes into a joint account, splits what is earned during the marriage 50/50, and credits a spouse who cuts back work for the family with a share of the other’s pay for each such year (15 percent in the draft).
  • The Children’s Contract puts the children’s fund ahead of any split and keeps the family home until the youngest finishes high school.

What to do

Before either of you meets a lawyer, write down what you want the agreement to do. Start from one of the three contracts or from the default rules with numbers filled in. Then each of you should take that draft to your own lawyer.
