Does your spouse’s debt become yours?

Eric SilverOctober 13, 2026

In most states, getting married doesn’t make you liable for what your spouse owes. A debt belongs to the people who signed for it. If your signature is on a loan or a card along with your spouse’s, it is your debt too. The exceptions to that rule are where the trouble is, and nearly all of them add liability rather than remove it.

The hospital and the widow

The first exception comes from an old rule called the doctrine of necessaries. It lets a creditor who supplied necessities to one spouse collect from the other. Today the necessity in question is nearly always medical care.

The typical case runs like this. One spouse is hospitalized for a long time and dies. The estate can’t cover the bill. The hospital then sues the surviving spouse, who may never have signed an admission form.

Medical debt is common enough that this isn’t a remote risk. In a 2022 KFF survey, 41 percent of adults reported some debt caused by medical or dental bills.

States have answered the widow’s question in three ways.

  • Abolished. Florida’s Supreme Court abolished the doctrine of necessaries in 1995.
  • Liability second. New Jersey’s Supreme Court held in 1980 that both spouses are liable, but the creditor has to go after the spouse who incurred the debt first. Only after that is exhausted can it turn to the other.
  • Liability from the start. Some states make both spouses liable without that order of collection, either under the common-law doctrine or under a statute that makes family expenses a joint obligation.

Illinois is one state with a family expense statute. It makes the expenses of the family chargeable on the property of both spouses.

Which group your state is in is worth knowing before a hospital stay, not after.

Community property: the shared pot

In a community property state, the question is less about you personally and more about the property the two of you share. California is the clearest example. There, the community estate is liable for a debt either spouse incurred, whether before the marriage or during it.

California has an exception worth knowing. A spouse’s earnings are protected from the other spouse’s premarital debts if they sit in an account the indebted spouse has no access to, holding nothing else from the community.

That exception came up in a story reported by Newsweek in May 2025. A man posted on Reddit that he had called off his wedding a few weeks before the date, after his fiancée refused a prenup and then told him she had $92,000 in credit-card debt. One commenter read the refusal as an attempt to leave him responsible for the balance. The post is a self-report, and none of its facts have been verified. On the law, though, even California would have let him keep his paycheck out of reach of her pre-wedding debt, provided he kept it apart. His real exposure was to spending after the wedding.

Joint signatures: the tax return and the loan

Signing jointly is the most direct way to take on a spouse’s debt.

A joint federal income tax return makes both signers jointly and severally liable for the tax. Either spouse can be made to pay all of it, including tax on income only the other one earned. Relief exists, called innocent spouse relief, but its test is demanding.

Student loans once offered a stranger version. Federal rules let married couples combine their student debt in a single joint consolidation loan, and each of them owed all of it. There was no way to separate the loan after a divorce. Congress passed the Joint Consolidation Loan Separation Act in October 2022 to allow a split.

A divorce doesn’t undo any of this. A decree can say which of you pays a joint card, but the lender never agreed to that, and it can still collect from whoever signed.

What you can do

Each of you lists every debt that carries your name, with an approximate balance. Trade lists, then compare them with your credit reports. Couples who do this usually find something one of them forgot, like an old store card or a loan co-signed for a relative years ago.

Before you sign a joint tax return, read page one and ask about any number that surprises you.

Then find out which property system your state uses on your state’s page, and read Article 5 of the default contract, which covers debts.
