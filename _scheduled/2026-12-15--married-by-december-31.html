Why December 31 decides your tax status for the whole year

Eric Silver · December 15, 2026

For federal income tax, your marital status is fixed at the close of the tax year. If you are married on December 31, you are treated as married for the entire year. If your divorce is final by December 31, you are treated as unmarried for the entire year. What happened earlier in the year doesn’t matter.

The rule is in section 7703(a) of the Internal Revenue Code. It turns one date into a decision about twelve months.

A wedding in December, or in January

For a couple planning a wedding near the turn of the year, the date has a tax effect. Some couples pay less tax filing jointly than they would as two single people. That is the marriage bonus. Others pay more, which is the marriage penalty. A couple who expect a bonus gain a full year of it by marrying in December. A couple who expect a penalty avoid a full year of it by waiting until January.

Which group you fall into depends on your incomes. The Boyters, described below, were two people with similar salaries, and marriage cost them money. The only way to know where you stand is to run the numbers both ways.

A divorce in December, or in January

Divorcing couples face the same question from the other side. Whether the decree lands before or after New Year’s can change who may file as head of household. It can also change who is responsible for what on a final joint return. People negotiating a divorce late in the year sometimes argue over the date for exactly this reason.

The couple who divorced for the tax year

The Boyters of Maryland tried to use the rule as a strategy. Both worked for the federal government, and they earned similar salaries. They worked out that marriage added a few thousand dollars to their annual tax bill.

So they arranged to be single on December 31. On December 8, 1975, they obtained a divorce in Haiti. They came home, filed their returns as two unmarried people, and married each other again early the following year. Late in 1976 they repeated the process, this time with a divorce in the Dominican Republic dated November 22, 1976. Again each filed as a single taxpayer, and then they wed each other once more after New Year’s.

The IRS challenged the returns. The Tax Court held that the foreign divorces were invalid under Maryland law. On that view, the couple had never stopped being married.

The federal appeals court for the Fourth Circuit, in Boyter v. Commissioner (1981), took a different path. It sent the case back to the Tax Court to decide another question: whether a divorce obtained only to change a couple’s tax status, and followed promptly by remarriage, ought to be ignored as a sham.

The appeals court’s question is the part worth remembering. The law doesn’t require pure motives for marrying. What draws scrutiny is a change in status whose only purpose is the tax result. A divorce followed shortly by remarriage to the same person is hard to explain any other way.

What you can do

If you are planning a wedding in November or December, find out whether marriage will raise or lower your combined federal income tax. An hour with a tax preparer comparing a joint return against two single returns will tell you. If the answer is a penalty, a January date saves you a year of it. If it’s a bonus, a December date earns you a year of it.

If you are in the middle of a divorce that could be finalized near the end of the year, raise the date with your lawyer and a tax preparer before it’s set. Ask how a decree in December compares with one in January for filing status, for head of household, and for any final joint return. The difference can matter to both of you, and it is easier to settle while the date is still open than to argue about afterward.

Don’t copy the Boyters. A divorce that exists only to be undone in January is the kind of arrangement the Fourth Circuit told the Tax Court to examine as a possible sham.
