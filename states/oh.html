Ohio

Your marriage contract, Ohio edition

Luck of the Draw score: 75 out of 100

Ohio starts from an equal split of marital property and leaves spousal support, including how long it lasts, to the judge's weighing of factors. Fee awards are left to what the judge finds equitable, and there is no way to remove a judge without cause. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Ohio ranks 25 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Ohio is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

The spouse who got the care pays first. Each married person must support himself or herself first, and the other spouse must help only if that person is unable; a creditor must first seek payment from the spouse who incurred the debt, and the statute does not make spouses jointly liable. If the debtor spouse has died, the creditor must first present its claim to that spouse's estate.
Ohio Rev. Code § 3103.03; Embassy Healthcare v. Bell, 155 Ohio St.3d 430, 2018-Ohio-4912

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

A surviving spouse who elects against the will takes the intestate share, capped at one-half of the net estate, or one-third if two or more of the decedent's children or their descendants survive; the share is measured against the net probate estate, not an augmented estate.
Ohio Rev. Code § 2106.01(C)

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under case law only for premarital agreements (Gross v. Gross); own statute for postnuptial agreements (R.C. 3103.061). A premarital agreement is valid if entered freely without fraud, duress, coercion or overreaching, with full disclosure or full knowledge of the other's property, and if its terms do not promote divorce; property terms are judged as of signing, but spousal support terms are also reviewed for conscionability at the time of divorce. Since March 23, 2023, spouses may make postnuptial agreements, valid if in writing, signed by both, entered freely, made with full disclosure or knowledge, and not promoting divorce.
Gross v. Gross, 11 Ohio St.3d 99 (1984); Ohio Rev. Code §§ 3103.06, 3103.061

What an agreement needs. A premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by the party to be charged under the statute of frauds; Ohio requires no notarization, witnesses, waiting period or independent counsel, but requires full disclosure or full knowledge and understanding of the other's property. Spousal support waivers are allowed, but a court may refuse to enforce one that is unconscionable at the time of divorce.
Ohio Rev. Code § 1335.05; Gross v. Gross, 11 Ohio St.3d 99 (1984); Ohio Rev. Code § 3103.061 (postnuptial)
