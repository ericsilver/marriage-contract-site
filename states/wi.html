Wisconsin

Your marriage contract, Wisconsin edition

55 of 100 on the Luck of the Draw Index

All states

Luck of the Draw score: 55 out of 100

Wisconsin presumes an equal property split, excludes fault from both property and maintenance, and treats equal income division as a starting point for maintenance. A party may substitute the assigned judge once without cause, and there is no jury. On the book’s scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Wisconsin ranks 8 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. Getting in

A 3-day wait. Apply to a county clerk in any county, with proof of identity, residence and birth. No license can issue within 3 days of application, though a clerk may shorten the wait for an extra fee of up to $25. The license is good for 60 days.
Wis. Stat. §§ 765.05, 765.08, 765.09, 765.12(2), 765.15

Who can marry you. Clergy who remain ordained, certain licentiates, judges, court commissioners and municipal judges may officiate, if at least 18. A couple may also marry themselves under the customs of a religious society to which either belongs. Two adult witnesses are required, or one if a party is on active military duty. The statute does not address online ordination.
Wis. Stat. § 765.16

Common-law marriage: Not available here. A couple cannot form a common-law marriage in Wisconsin; it was abolished in 1917. Courts generally apply the law of the place where a marriage was contracted, but Wisconsin residents who vacationed in a common-law state did not become married, and a statute voids marriages residents make elsewhere to evade Wisconsin’s prohibitions.
Wis. Stat. §§ 765.16, 765.04; Van Schaick v. Van Schaick, 256 Wis. 214 (1949); Watts v. Watts, 137 Wis. 2d 506, 405 N.W.2d 305 (1987); Xiong v. Xiong, 2002 WI App 110, 255 Wis. 2d 693, 648 N.W.2d 900

The property system

Wisconsin is one of nine community property jurisdictions. Community property. What either of you earns during the marriage belongs to both of you as it’s earned, and it’s generally split in half at the end. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

What counts as marital. All spouses’ property is presumed marital, and that status lasts until divorce. At divorce the judge presumes an equal split of everything except gifts and inheritances, so premarital property is divisible. Gifts and inheritances stay with the recipient unless that would cause hardship. Growth in them caused by the spouses’ efforts is divisible.
Wis. Stat. §§ 766.01(7)-(8), 766.31(1)-(2), 767.61(2)-(3); Schorer v. Schorer, 177 Wis. 2d 387, 501 N.W.2d 916 (Ct. App. 1993)

An inheritance that touches a joint account

Putting it in joint names usually makes it marital. Inherited money deposited in a joint bank account, even briefly, is presumed a gift to the marriage, and so is moving property into joint tenancy. To keep it exempt, the owner must trace it and rebut the presumption with evidence that no gift was intended. Commingled inheritance loses its exempt status, though the judge may consider its origin and may divide exempt property to prevent hardship.
Finley v. Finley, 2002 WI App 144, 256 Wis. 2d 508, 648 N.W.2d 536; Derr v. Derr, 2005 WI App 63, 280 Wis. 2d 681; Steinmann v. Steinmann, 2008 WI 43, 309 Wis. 2d 29, 749 N.W.2d 145; Wright v. Wright, 2008 WI App 21, 307 Wis. 2d 156, 747 N.W.2d 690; Schwartz v. Linders, 145 Wis. 2d 258, 426 N.W.2d 97 (Ct. App. 1988); Wis. Stat. § 767.61(2)(b)

Your spouse’s medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. Each spouse has an equal duty to support the other and neither is presumed primarily liable for support expenses, so a spouse is responsible for the other’s necessary medical expenses, and the creditor may collect from all marital property and all other property of the obligated spouse. Wisconsin is a marital (community) property state: an obligation incurred in the interest of the marriage or family may be satisfied from all marital property and the incurring spouse’s other property.
Wis. Stat. §§ 765.001(2), 766.55(2)(a)-(b); St. Mary’s Hospital Medical Center v. Brody, 186 Wis. 2d 100, 519 N.W.2d 706 (Ct. App. 1994)

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse keeps his or her undivided one-half interest in each item of marital property. In addition, the survivor may elect up to 50 percent of the augmented deferred marital property estate, which covers property acquired during the marriage before Wisconsin’s marital property law applied that would have been marital property if acquired under it (Wisconsin’s counterpart to quasi-community property).
Wis. Stat. §§ 861.01(1), 861.018(2), 861.02

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under own statute (Wisconsin Marital Property Act, based on the Uniform Marital Property Act), Wis. Stat. § 766.58, plus § 767.61(3)(L) and case law. A marital property agreement made before or during marriage is unenforceable if unconscionable when made, not signed voluntarily, or signed without fair and reasonable disclosure and without notice of the other’s finances. At divorce a property agreement binds the court unless its terms are inequitable, and an agreement fair at execution may be inequitable at divorce if circumstances changed significantly after signing so that it no longer meets the parties’ reasonable expectations. The same rules govern agreements made during marriage.
Wis. Stat. §§ 766.58(6), 767.61(3)(L); Button v. Button, 131 Wis. 2d 84, 388 N.W.2d 546 (1986)

What an agreement needs. A marital property agreement must be a document signed by both spouses (or both intended spouses, effective on marriage) and needs no consideration; the statute does not require notarization, witnesses, a waiting period or independent counsel, and one lawyer for both or counsel for only one party does not by itself make it unconscionable. A spousal support waiver may not leave a spouse without necessary and adequate support during the marriage, and if it would make a spouse eligible for public assistance at divorce or death the court may order support. The agreement may be recorded with the register of deeds.
Wis. Stat. § 766.58(1), (5), (8), (9), (11)

Children (Article 8)

Child support: percentage of income. Wisconsin uses a percentage of the paying parent’s income: 17 percent for one child, 25 for two, 29 for three, 31 for four and 34 for five or more. The judge may deviate if that is unfair. Support runs to 18, or 19 while pursuing a high school diploma. College support comes only from the parents’ agreement.
Wis. Admin. Code DCF 150.035(2); Wis. Stat. § 767.511(1j), (1m), (4); Bliwas v. Bliwas, 47 Wis. 2d 635, 178 N.W.2d 35 (1970)

Moving away with a child. A parent moving with a child 100 driving miles or more from the other parent must first ask the court, and the child cannot move before the initial hearing. If contested, best interests decide and the moving parent bears the burden, unless a presumption applies for abuse or an objector’s unused placement. Parents already over 100 miles apart give 60 days’ notice.
Wis. Stat. § 767.481(1), (2), (4)

Getting out (Article 2)

How long you must live here before filing: six months. One spouse must have been a genuine Wisconsin resident for at least six months, and a resident of the filing county for at least 30 days, before the case starts. The rule for divorce does not depend on where the couple married or where the grounds arose.
Wis. Stat. § 767.301

How long it takes. One spouse can obtain a divorce by stating under oath that the marriage is irretrievably broken. If the couple has not lived apart 12 months, the judge weighs the prospect of reconciliation and may continue the case 30 to 60 days. The final hearing cannot occur until 120 days after service, absent an emergency order.
Wis. Stat. §§ 767.315, 767.335, 767.35

The legal minimum for a spouse who moves here alone and files as soon as allowed, with no minor children and the other spouse objecting, is about 300 days from arrival to a final decree (180 days of residence + 120-day wait after service). Courts often take longer.
