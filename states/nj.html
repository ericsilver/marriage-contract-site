New Jersey

Your marriage contract, New Jersey edition

Luck of the Draw score: 75 out of 100

New Jersey judges divide marital property and set the amount of alimony by weighing factors, with no equal-split starting point. Alimony for marriages under 20 years is capped at the length of the marriage except in exceptional circumstances, and marital fault counts only when egregious or economically harmful. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” New Jersey ranks 25 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

New Jersey is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

The spouse who got the care pays first. Each spouse is liable for the other's necessary expenses, but a creditor must first collect from the income and property of the spouse who incurred the debt and may reach the other spouse only if those resources are insufficient.
Jersey Shore Medical Center-Fitkin Hospital v. Estate of Baum, 84 N.J. 137 (1980)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect one-third of the augmented estate, which reaches certain non-probate transfers; there is no election if either spouse had filed a complaint for divorce or dissolution that had not been dismissed.
N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1 (as amended by P.L.2023, c.238, approved and effective Jan. 8, 2024)

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), as amended 2013. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if the challenger proves by clear and convincing evidence that it was signed involuntarily, or that it was unconscionable when signed because the challenger lacked full and fair disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing, lacked adequate knowledge, or neither consulted independent counsel nor waived counsel in writing. The 2013 amendment deleted the former test of unconscionability at the time enforcement is sought. Mid-marriage (postnuptial) agreements fall outside the Act; they are enforceable but closely scrutinized and must be fair and equitable.
N.J.S.A. 37:2-38 (P.L.2013, c.72); Pacelli v. Pacelli, 319 N.J. Super. 185 (App. Div. 1999)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing, signed by both parties, with a statement of assets annexed; the statute requires no notarization, witnesses or waiting period. An agreement can be set aside if a party lacked full and fair disclosure without a written waiver, or neither consulted independent counsel nor waived counsel in writing; the agreement may modify or eliminate spousal support, and the statute has no public-assistance override.
N.J.S.A. 37:2-33, 37:2-34(d), 37:2-38(c)
