Oklahoma

Your marriage contract, Oklahoma edition

Luck of the Draw score: 85 out of 100

Oklahoma's statute tells judges only to divide jointly acquired property justly and reasonably and to award alimony they think reasonable, so nearly everything is left to the judge. Premarital property stays with its owner, and fee awards follow what is just and proper. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Oklahoma ranks 36 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Oklahoma is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. Spouses are jointly and severally liable for debts incurred for necessaries furnished to either spouse, unless a law or court order provides otherwise.
Okla. Stat. tit. 43, § 209.1 (added 2000)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

For decedents dying on or after July 1, 1985, a will may not leave the surviving spouse less than an undivided one-half interest in property acquired by the joint industry of the spouses during the marriage, and the spouse may elect that one-half in place of the will's gifts; the statute operates against the will and does not by its terms reach non-probate transfers.
Okla. Stat. tit. 84, § 44(B)

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under case law only (no UPAA); statutes recognize a 'valid antenuptial contract in writing'. An antenuptial agreement that is just and reasonable and entered freely without fraud is enforced, including a waiver of alimony and property division on divorce; if the agreement is unfair on its face, the party relying on it must show full and fair disclosure and good faith. Postnuptial agreements that alter divorce property rights, including post-marriage amendments to a prenup, have been held unenforceable because no statute authorizes them.
Hudson v. Hudson, 350 P.2d 596 (Okla. 1960); Freeman v. Freeman, 565 P.2d 365 (Okla. 1977); In re Estate of Cobb, 305 P.2d 1028 (Okla. 1956); Hendrick v. Hendrick, 1999 OK CIV APP 15, 976 P.2d 1071

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing to control property division on divorce or to limit a will; no statute requires notarization, witnesses, a waiting period or independent counsel. Disclosure matters because an agreement that looks unfair shifts to its proponent the burden of proving full disclosure; alimony waivers have been enforced.
Okla. Stat. tit. 43, § 121(B); Okla. Stat. tit. 84, § 44; Hudson v. Hudson, 350 P.2d 596 (Okla. 1960); In re Estate of Cobb, 305 P.2d 1028 (Okla. 1956)
