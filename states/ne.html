Nebraska

Your marriage contract, Nebraska edition

Luck of the Draw score: 75 out of 100

Nebraska judges divide marital property and set alimony by what is reasonable, usually giving each spouse between one-third and one-half of the marital estate, and they may not weigh who was at fault. Fee awards are left to the judge's discretion. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Nebraska ranks 25 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Nebraska is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

The spouse who got the care pays first. By statute a married woman's non-exempt property is liable for debts for necessaries furnished to the family only after execution against the husband has been returned unsatisfied; case law holds the husband primarily liable and treats a husband's medical care as a family necessary for which the wife may be liable on that secondary basis.
Neb. Rev. Stat. § 42-201; Nichol v. Clema, 188 Neb. 74 (1972); Scott v. House, 93 Neb. 325 (1913)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect up to one-half of the augmented estate (1969 UPC model), which adds back to the probate estate transfers during marriage with retained enjoyment or revocation power, joint-and-survivorship property, and gifts within three years of death above $3,000 per donee per year, and counts the survivor's own property derived from the decedent.
Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 30-2313, 30-2314

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 42-1001 to -1011. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if the challenger proves it was not signed voluntarily, or that it was unconscionable when executed and the challenger lacked fair and reasonable disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing, and had no adequate knowledge of the other's finances.
Neb. Rev. Stat. § 42-1006

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; no notarization, witnesses, waiting period, or counsel requirement applies, though courts weigh proximity to the wedding and access to independent counsel in deciding voluntariness. If a support waiver would make a spouse eligible for public assistance at separation or divorce, the court may order support to the extent needed to avoid that.
Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 42-1003, 42-1006(2)
