Louisiana

Your marriage contract, Louisiana edition

Luck of the Draw score: 40 out of 100

Louisiana leaves little to the judge on property: community property is split at equal net value, and alimony is capped at one-third of the payor's net income and barred for a spouse at fault. Each side generally pays its own lawyer. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Louisiana ranks 3 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Louisiana is one of nine community property jurisdictions. Community property. What either of you earns during the marriage belongs to both of you as it's earned, and it's generally split in half at the end. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. Under the community regime, an obligation incurred by either spouse during marriage is presumed to be a community obligation and may be satisfied from all community property and from the separate property of the spouse who incurred it, but not from the other spouse's separate property; spouses under a separation-of-property regime are solidarily liable for necessaries either incurs for himself or the family.
La. Civ. Code arts. 2345, 2360, 2361, 2372

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Louisiana has no elective share; the survivor keeps his or her half of the community, the decedent's half passes by will, and if there is no will it goes to the survivor when there are no descendants or to the descendants subject to the survivor's usufruct until death or remarriage. A spouse left poor compared with a rich decedent may claim the marital portion: one-fourth of the succession in ownership if there are no children, one-fourth in usufruct if there are three or fewer children, capped at $1 million.
La. Civ. Code arts. 889, 890, 2432, 2434, 3526

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under its own statute (Louisiana Civil Code matrimonial-agreement articles, arts. 2328-2333). A matrimonial agreement is a contract enforceable on the same terms as other contracts and open to rescission only on the ordinary contract grounds (vices of consent at formation); a premarital waiver of post-divorce periodic support is enforceable. Spouses may make or change a matrimonial agreement during marriage, but modifying or ending the regime requires a joint petition and a court finding that it serves their best interests, except during the first year after moving to Louisiana.
La. Civ. Code arts. 2328, 2329; McAlpine v. McAlpine, 679 So. 2d 85 (La. 1996)

What an agreement needs. A matrimonial agreement must be made by authentic act (before a notary and two witnesses) or by an act under private signature duly acknowledged by the spouses; a mid-marriage agreement changing the regime needs court approval, and spouses may not renounce or alter the marital portion or the order of succession. No waiting period or independent-counsel rule appears in the articles.
La. Civ. Code arts. 2329, 2330, 2331
