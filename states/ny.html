New York

Your marriage contract, New York edition

Luck of the Draw score: 60 out of 100

New York sets post-divorce maintenance by a statutory formula up to an income cap, gives an advisory schedule for how long it lasts, and presumes the less monied spouse gets counsel fees. Judges still divide marital property by weighing factors with no equal-split rule. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” New York ranks 13 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

New York is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

The spouse who got the care pays first. The common-law necessaries doctrine applies to both spouses, but the spouse who received the goods or services is primarily liable; a creditor must first pursue that spouse, may reach the other spouse only if the recipient's resources are insufficient, and must show the necessaries were furnished on the other spouse's credit and that the other spouse can pay.
Medical Business Associates, Inc. v. Steiner, 183 A.D.2d 86 (2d Dep't 1992)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect the greater of $50,000 (or the whole net estate if smaller) or one-third of the net estate, and the estate for this purpose includes testamentary substitutes such as joint and payable-on-death accounts, jointly held property, retained-interest or revocable transfers, gifts within one year of death and certain retirement benefits.
N.Y. Est. Powers & Trusts Law § 5-1.1-A (decedents dying on or after Sept. 1, 1992)

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under its own statute (DRL § 236(B)(3)); New York did not adopt the UPAA. An agreement made before or during marriage is valid if written, subscribed and acknowledged, and a court may set it aside for fraud, duress, or manifest unfairness produced by overreaching. Maintenance terms get a second look: they must be fair and reasonable when made and not unconscionable at the time of final judgment. Postnuptial agreements are governed by the same statute and standard.
N.Y. Dom. Rel. Law § 236(B)(3); Christian v. Christian, 42 N.Y.2d 63 (1977)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing, subscribed by both parties, and acknowledged or proven in the manner required to record a deed; an unacknowledged agreement is unenforceable. No statute requires a waiting period, independent counsel or a disclosure schedule; maintenance terms must be fair when made and not unconscionable at judgment, spouses cannot contract away support so that one is likely to become a public charge, and child-related terms are subject to DRL § 240.
N.Y. Dom. Rel. Law § 236(B)(3); N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-311; Matisoff v. Dobi, 90 N.Y.2d 127 (1997)
