Montana

Your marriage contract, Montana edition

Luck of the Draw score: 70 out of 100

Montana judges may divide everything either spouse owns, including property from before the marriage or inherited, and set maintenance as they find just, but they may not consider marital misconduct. Each party may substitute the assigned judge once without giving a reason. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Montana ranks 18 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Montana is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. A spouse is not liable for the other's debts solely because of the marriage, except that expenses for necessities of the family and the education of the children are chargeable on the property of both spouses or either of them, and the spouses may be sued jointly or separately for them.
Mont. Code Ann. § 40-2-106(1)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

Montana follows the 2008 UPC: the surviving spouse may elect 50 percent of the marital-property portion of the augmented estate, where that portion is the whole augmented estate multiplied by a percentage that rises with marriage length (3 percent under one year to 100 percent at 15 years or more), with a $75,000 supplemental floor; the augmented estate includes the net probate estate, nonprobate transfers to others and to the spouse, and the spouse's own property.
Mont. Code Ann. §§ 72-2-232, 72-2-233 (2019 Mont. Laws ch. 313)

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), Mont. Code Ann. §§ 40-2-601 to -610. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if the challenger proves it was not signed voluntarily, or that it was unconscionable when executed and the challenger lacked fair and reasonable disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing, and had no adequate knowledge of the other's finances. Spouses may contract with each other about property, subject to the rules governing persons in confidential relations, so postnuptial agreements are allowed under that fiduciary standard; a marital agreement waiving elective-share rights has its own statutory test.
Mont. Code Ann. §§ 40-2-608, 40-2-301, 72-2-243

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties and is enforceable without consideration; no notarization, witnesses, waiting period, or counsel requirement applies. If a support waiver would make a spouse eligible for public assistance at separation or divorce, the court may order support to the extent needed to avoid that. A waiver of elective-share rights (before or after marriage) fails if the surviving spouse lacked access to independent legal representation, lacked a plain-language explanation of the rights waived when unrepresented, or did not receive adequate financial disclosure.
Mont. Code Ann. §§ 40-2-604, 40-2-608(2), 72-2-243
