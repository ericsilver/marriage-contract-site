Connecticut

Your marriage contract, Connecticut edition

Luck of the Draw score: 100 out of 100

Connecticut leaves nearly every money question in a divorce to the judge, who can divide all property of either spouse, including premarital and inherited property, and sets alimony from a list of factors with no formula or time limit. Fault counts in both property and alimony, fee awards are discretionary, and the case is tried to a single judge with no jury and no way to remove that judge without cause. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Connecticut ranks 51 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Connecticut is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. By statute each spouse has a joint duty to support the family, and both are liable for reasonable and necessary physician and dentist services, hospital expenses of either spouse or a minor child living in the family, the rent of their home, and articles that went to the family's support. Exceptions apply during a separation in which reasonable support was provided and for a spouse who abandoned the other without cause.
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 46b-37(b), (d), (e)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may elect a statutory share equal to a life estate in one-third of the property passing under the will, after debts. The share reaches only the probate estate, not non-probate assets.
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 45a-436(a)

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

Two looks, under Connecticut Premarital Agreement Act (own variant of the UPAA), Conn. Gen. Stat. §§ 46b-36a to 46b-36j, for agreements signed on or after Oct. 1, 1995. A premarital agreement is unenforceable if not signed voluntarily, if unconscionable when signed or when enforcement is sought, if the challenger did not receive fair and reasonable disclosure of the other's property, obligations and income, or if the challenger had no reasonable opportunity to consult independent counsel. Postnuptial agreements get stricter scrutiny: they must satisfy contract principles, be fair and equitable when signed, and not be unconscionable at dissolution.
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 46b-36g(a); Bedrick v. Bedrick, 300 Conn. 691 (2011)

What an agreement needs. The agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties and needs no consideration; there is no notary, witness or waiting-period requirement. Each party must receive fair and reasonable disclosure of the amount, character and value of the other's property, obligations and income and have a reasonable opportunity to consult independent counsel, and a court may order support despite a waiver that would leave a spouse on public assistance.
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 46b-36c; § 46b-36g(a)(3), (a)(4), (b)
