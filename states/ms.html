Mississippi

Your marriage contract, Mississippi edition

Luck of the Draw score: 95 out of 100

Mississippi chancellors divide marital property and set alimony by weighing court-made factor lists, and adultery or other misconduct can count against a spouse in both. Fee awards are limited to spouses who cannot afford their own lawyer. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Mississippi ranks 45 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Mississippi is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Generally, you don't owe your spouse's bills. Absent an express agreement or an agency relationship, one spouse is not liable to a third party for the other spouse's debts, including medical bills.
Govan v. Medical Credit Services, Inc., 621 So. 2d 928, 930 (Miss. 1993); McLain v. West Side Bone & Joint Center, 656 So. 2d 119, 121 (Miss. 1995)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

A spouse who renounces the will (or is left nothing) takes the intestate share, which is a child's share if the decedent left descendants, capped at one-half of the estate; the right is lost, fully or proportionally, to the extent the survivor's own separate property equals the value of that share; the share is computed on the probate estate.
Miss. Code Ann. §§ 91-5-25, 91-5-27, 91-5-29, 91-1-7

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under case law only (Hensley 1988; Mabus 2003; Sanderson 2014). A prenuptial agreement is enforceable like any other contract if it was fair in its execution with full disclosure (procedural conscionability); courts also review substantive unconscionability, measured by the circumstances existing when the agreement was made, and do not relieve a party of a provision that became more onerous than anticipated because of later events.
Sanderson v. Sanderson, 170 So. 3d 430 (Miss. 2014); Sanderson v. Sanderson, 245 So. 3d 421 (Miss. 2018); Mabus v. Mabus, 890 So. 2d 806 (Miss. 2003); Estate of Hensley v. Estate of Hensley, 524 So. 2d 325 (Miss. 1988)

What an agreement needs. An agreement made upon consideration of marriage must be in writing and signed (statute of frauds); there is no notarization, witness, or waiting-period requirement, full financial disclosure (or independent knowledge) is required, and independent counsel is not required though the opportunity to consult counsel weighs in the procedural analysis. No statute limits alimony waivers.
Miss. Code Ann. § 15-3-1(b); Sanderson v. Sanderson, 170 So. 3d 430 (Miss. 2014); Smith v. Smith, 656 So. 2d 1143, 1147 (Miss. 1995)
