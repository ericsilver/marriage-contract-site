Virginia

Your marriage contract, Virginia edition

Luck of the Draw score: 95 out of 100

Virginia leaves the property split and the amount and length of spousal support to the judge's weighing of listed factors, including the reasons the marriage ended. Adultery bars permanent support unless the judge finds that denial would be a manifest injustice. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” Virginia ranks 45 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

Virginia is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. The common-law doctrine of necessaries applies equally to both spouses unless they are permanently living separate and apart, but no spouse is liable for health care furnished to a patient spouse who dies first; apart from necessaries, a spouse is not responsible for the other's contracts. A judgment lien under this section cannot attach to a residence held as tenants by the entirety.
Va. Code § 55.1-202 (as amended by 2023 Va. Acts ch. 798)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

For deaths on or after January 1, 2017, the surviving spouse may elect 50 percent of the marital-property portion of the augmented estate (probate estate, non-probate transfers and the survivor's own property); the marital-property portion rises from 3 percent for a marriage under one year to 100 percent after 15 years.
Va. Code §§ 64.2-308.1, 64.2-308.3, 64.2-308.4

Chapter 9 covers what a will can and can't take away from a spouse.

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983), modified; Premarital Agreement Act, Va. Code §§ 20-147 to 20-155. A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if the challenger proves he or she did not sign voluntarily, or that it was unconscionable when signed and the challenger received no fair and reasonable disclosure and did not waive disclosure in writing; recitations in the agreement are presumed correct. Marital agreements (postnups) are subject to the same conditions and take effect when signed.
Va. Code §§ 20-151, 20-155

What an agreement needs. A premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties and needs no consideration; the statute does not require notarization, witnesses, a waiting period or independent counsel. Parties may contract about spousal support, and the Virginia statute has no public-assistance override. A marital agreement need not be written if its terms are in a court order endorsed by counsel or the parties, or recorded by a court reporter and affirmed by the parties on the record.
Va. Code §§ 20-149, 20-150, 20-151, 20-155
