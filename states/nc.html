North Carolina

Your marriage contract, North Carolina edition

Luck of the Draw score: 55 out of 100

North Carolina starts from an equal split of marital property and keeps marital fault out of property division. In alimony, fault matters by statute, including a bar on alimony for a dependent spouse who committed illicit sexual behavior, and either side can ask a jury to decide the misconduct question. On the book's scale, 0 is a contract you can read and 100 is one that mostly says “ask the judge.” North Carolina ranks 8 of 51, counting from the state that leaves the least to the judge. The property system

North Carolina is one of forty-one equitable distribution jurisdictions. Equitable distribution. Each of you owns what's in your own name during the marriage. At a divorce a judge divides the marital property as the judge finds equitable, which is not a promise of half. Chapter 11 of the book covers what that means, and Chapter 22 covers what happens to it when you move.

Your spouse's medical bills (Article 5)

Both of you can be liable from the first dollar. Each spouse is liable for the other's necessary medical expenses; the provider need only show necessary services to one spouse, that the defendant was married to that spouse at the time, and that the bill is unpaid, with no requirement to pursue the patient spouse first. A spouse avoids liability only if the couple was separated and the provider had actual notice of the separation.
North Carolina Baptist Hospitals, Inc. v. Harris, 319 N.C. 347 (1987); Forsyth Memorial Hospital, Inc. v. Chisholm, 342 N.C. 616 (1996)

Chapter 8 of the book explains the three ways states handle this.

If one of you dies (Article 9)

The surviving spouse may claim a share of Total Net Assets set by length of marriage (15 percent under 5 years, 25 percent for 5 to 10 years, 33 percent for 10 to 15 years, 50 percent at 15 years or more), less property already passing to the spouse; Total Net Assets include non-probate property such as revocable trusts, payable-on-death accounts, survivorship property and part of life insurance.
N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 30-3.1, 30-3.2

Prenups and postnups (Article 12)

One look, under UPAA (1983). A premarital agreement is unenforceable only if the challenger proves it was signed involuntarily, or was unconscionable when signed and the challenger lacked fair disclosure, did not waive disclosure in writing, and lacked adequate knowledge. Contracts between spouses during marriage are valid if not against public policy, but one affecting real property or dividing marital property must be written and acknowledged before a notary or other certifying officer.
N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 52B-7, 52-10, 50-20(d)

What an agreement needs. A premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; chapter 52B requires no notarization, witnesses, waiting period or independent counsel, and disclosure matters through the enforcement test. The agreement may modify or eliminate spousal support, but a court may order support if the waiver would leave a dependent spouse eligible for public assistance.
N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 52B-3, 52B-4(a)(4), 52B-7(b)
